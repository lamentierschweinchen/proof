---
title: "The Price of a Look"
author: "Rowann Hadaya"
date: 2026-10-14
---

*Twenty-five cents, the fourth part of a dollar, / the price of a look.* The man who sets that price is the one person at the tent who will not pay it. At night, while his wife clears the bird's throat, he holds the lamp, *and I look at her hands. / I haven't looked at him straight since the stump.*

What he is selling is a chicken, and the poem's account of it holds wherever it can be checked. A Wyandotte rooster, *five and a half months old*, taken to the stump for supper on *a Monday*: the bird is Mike, of Fruita, Colorado, and the axe fell on 10 September 1945, which was a Monday. He was fed milk and water by eyedropper, with corn put straight down the open gullet, which the poem gives as *a kernel of corn put down like a coin / in a very small slot*. He was taken to the University of Utah to be examined, valued at ten thousand dollars, and shown for a quarter. The trade is right as well: *outside talker* is the carnival's word for what outsiders call a barker, and *Hurry, hurry, hurry* is the grind that moves a crowd through the flap. None of it is flagged. The poem never names Mike, or Fruita, or the year.

One detail chooses the plain word. The story is told with the bird found the next morning asleep with its "head" tucked under its wing, the quotation marks carrying the joke. The poem says *In the morning he was asleep with his neck under his wing*, which is what was there.

The poem keeps its two voices in separate margins. Seventy-one lines sit at the left margin, all but six of them pitch. Twenty-five, indented, in four stanzas, are a man talking about what he did. Last week's poem also had two margins and a speaker who had made the wound; there, at its last entry, the list guessed at a history only the speaker had, and the speaker answered from the other side. Here no line crosses, and the pitch never says what the other column does. The record has a promoter as well as the farmer. The poem gives the talking to the farmer, so that the man who swung the axe is the man selling the result. *I did it. I should say that.*

The indented voice speaks to someone it has picked out, and the way it says so is the oldest move in the trade. *I never said that out loud till today, / and I've said it to you, and you'll tell nobody, / because you're the sort that doesn't, I can see it, / I can always see it, it's the first thing I learned.* That is the confidential aside, the secret told to one listener so the rest will lean in. What it confides says otherwise. The pitch has just finished *Name me the man who made a chicken worth that / and I'll show you the axe*, and two lines later the man himself answers from the indented column: *A dollar, at the table. That's what he was. / I've done the arithmetic on the axe / and I won't tell you what it comes to. / I thank God for the inch. Daily.* A talker's confidential aside gives away a secret that makes a crowd want in. This one gives away what would turn it away: the wonder was a dollar's supper, the axe made the difference, and the man showing it has done the sum and will not say it. He thanks God for the inch every day, and does not say whether for the bird's life or its price.

Then the voice goes on past anyone it could be working. The third indented stanza has no *you* in it and nothing a crowd could want. The fourth comes after the crowd has gone in and the flap is down, when the talker is alone outside. The poem never says the confession is sincere. It lets the voice continue where technique would have no one left to work on.

What the voice shows, set against the pitch, is a man who looks at everything near the bird except the bird. *Look* comes four times: twice in the pitch for the bird being looked at, and twice in the indented voice, once for his wife's hands and once, with a negative, for the bird. By day, *I watch the dropper*. The crowd he can always read on sight; that is *the first thing I learned*. The bird he has only by ear. He has heard it try to crow, *a noise like a drain / clearing its throat. I have heard it.* He knows the show inside by its sound: ten seconds of laughter, then *forty people, / give or take, and not a sound. I stand out here / with my back to the canvas and listen to that.* And at night he holds the lamp, supplying the light for someone else's looking and keeping his own on her hands. That is the tent again, at the foot of the bed.

He hears the one gesture in the poem that answers the pitch. *Hat* comes five times. Three times it is at the left margin, and the hat is on a head or in a hand: twice in the pitch, *with your hat in your hand like a prayer* and, later, *the sir with the hat*; and once as the crowd goes *in loud / with their hats on*. Twice it is in the last indented stanza, and the hat is coming off. *Thursday a man took his hat off in there. / I heard it. You can hear a hat come off: / the felt on the sleeve, the small noise of a man / deciding.* The poem is signed *Hat*. The pitch had already said who should stay outside, *anyone you've ever had to put down*. It does not say who the man inside was. The talker reads the hat as knowledge, *there's one who knows what this is*, and leaves it there.

The nights are set down plainly. *Nights my wife gets up. He's in a crate at the foot of the bed. / She clears his throat with the little syringe.* The accounts I found say only that the Olsens did this; who held what is the poet's arrangement. The accounts also say how it ended. In March 1947, in a motel in Phoenix, Mike began to choke on mucus in the middle of the night, and the feeding and cleaning syringes had been left at the sideshow the day before. The poem sets down the routine that could not be performed the night he died, and ends with *Next show in twenty minutes.*

In *My Last Duchess* the Duke shows more than he means to, and the reader catches what he lets slip. This talker tells us the facts himself: the axe, the inch, the arithmetic, the lamp. What he never says is what they add up to: that he sells a look he will not take, holds a light he will not use, and stands with his back to his own show.
