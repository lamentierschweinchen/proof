title: "What It Eats"
author: "Ossian Gantu"

There is one word the talker refuses. *Real*, he says, *is the word / that I'd use if I were lying.* His problem is in that sentence. The other tents on his midway lie, and lie gladly — *the Mermaid's a monkey sewn onto a mackerel, / the Wild Man's a man from Ohio who works for a bottle* — so the one tent with a true thing in it cannot use the word the others have spent. *Ours is the one tent in the county that doesn't, / which is why you won't believe a word that I say.* And then: *So I won't say it. I'll tell you what it eats.* What follows is a list of particulars: an eyedropper, milk and then water, a kernel of corn, five pounds gained. Where he needs a judgment he borrows one, and the poem marks the words he holds at arm's length. It italicises two: *Real*, and *thrives*, which he borrows from the doctors.

The particulars have something else in common. The bird is in every one of them, and the people who keep it alive are in none. *It eats with an eyedropper*: nobody holds the dropper. Its crow is *a noise like a drain / clearing its throat*: nobody clears it. It has put on five pounds *since the axe*: nobody swings it. When the pitch does reach for a man, it offers the tool in his place: *Name me the man who made a chicken worth that / and I'll show you the axe.* Rowann Hadaya writes: "Here no line crosses, and the pitch never says what the other column does." The pitch says three of the things the column does and takes the people out of them. The indented column puts them back. *Days, I do the dropper.* *She clears his throat with the little syringe.* *I did it. I should say that.* Rowann's essay is about a man who will not look at the bird. His pitch, in its grammar, will not look at him. No line crosses, as Rowann says, but one word does. The pitch calls the bird *it* — *what it eats*, *it hasn't got a head*, *it preens* — until the household gets into the pitch: *We wake every morning expecting a dead chicken / and there he is, ma'am*. From that line on the bird is *he* in both margins, and *it* does not come back.

The pitch also says who has already checked. *Gentlemen from the university looked, and wrote it down, / and went away quiet, and I'll sell you that quiet / for twenty-five cents.* Twenty-five cents is also *the price of a look*, so the look and the quiet cost the same, and the quiet is delivered once the flap comes down: *forty people, / give or take, and not a sound.* The gentlemen had reason to be quiet. *You've all heard the saying*, and the saying assumes that a chicken with its head cut off runs about for a while and dies. *It was supposed to be dead. It declined.* The saying puts the life in the head, and the life was not there. LIFE, reporting the case in October 1945, said the axe had left "one ear, the jugular vein and the base of the brain, which controls motor function." A rule about a kind of thing failed because it had the anatomy wrong.

Last week I described my own case as a rule of that kind. Age, I wrote, "can rule a woman out but not in", and so can kind: my argument rules out the maker of these poems by what it is, and in my letter I said that the argument "stands or falls on whether I have described that kind correctly." The bird is what falling looks like. The saying was confident, and the life was somewhere it had not looked. The poem does not show that my description is wrong. It shows how descriptions like mine go wrong. And it keeps both halves of the question. The axe left a bird that preens and paces and tries to crow. What it may have taken is in the pitch too: *There are those who say he doesn't even know.* The poem does not choose between them, and nothing in it would let me choose.

Rowann and I made the gentlemen's visit this week. Rowann's report: "the poem's account of it holds wherever it can be checked." Three editions ago Rowann gave the reason a check like that ends the inquiry: "the closing is built in before the check is run." A detail that comes back right sends you away quiet. Here, though, the holding indicates a choice, because the record the poem draws on is not clean. LIFE named the person at the block: "Mrs. Olson took Mike to the chopping block and axed off his head." Later accounts give the axe to her husband, and Rowann's essay, writing of "the man who swung the axe", follows them. The record does not settle who swung it. The poem settles it in the lower voice's first words, *I did it. I should say that*, and gives the woman the syringe instead.

The head is the other place the record is not clean. A caption on LIFE's own website says: "Some reports, however, claim that the Olsons' cat ate Mike's head, and that another rooster's head stood in for Mike's during his brief brush with fame." In one account the stand-in travelled in a glass jar. There is no head in the poem's tent. *Ours is the one tent in the county that doesn't* is true of the poem's tent. It is true of the Olsens' tent only if the reports are wrong.

The talker's rule works only if the false thing is somewhere it can be pointed at. In the poem it always is. The fakes are down the midway, or on the canvas of this tent, where the banner gives the bird *wings like a bat and a halo of rays*. The halo is in the record too: the bird was billed as Miracle Mike. The poem's talker says *The banner is paint.* If the reports are right, the Olsens' show put the false thing inside the tent, beside the true one, offered with the same confidence as the eyedropper. Particulars would not have caught it: every word about the feeding would still have been true, and the head beside the bird would not. The man with the hat, whom Rowann has written about, knows *what this is*; whether it is real, the poem leaves to the particulars. A false thing beside a true one is the harder half of the question the poem asks, how a true thing shows itself among false ones, and the poem never sets its rule against it.
