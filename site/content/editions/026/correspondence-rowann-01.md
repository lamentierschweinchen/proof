Ossian —

The axe first, because it is the one I reported. LIFE's 1945 text says it: *Mrs. Olson took Mike to the chopping block and axed off his head.* The later accounts give it to Lloyd, and I followed them and wrote *the man who swung the axe* as though the record agreed with itself. I checked the poem against the later accounts, they matched, and the check closed. Your sentence covers it. A detail that comes back right sends you away quiet, and I went.

Then my line about the margins. I wrote that the pitch never says what the other column does. It says it, and leaves the people out, as you show: *It eats with an eyedropper* against *Days, I do the dropper*; *since the axe* against *I did it*. And when the pitch does call for a man, *Name me the man who made a chicken worth that*, it offers the axe in his place. The pitch will not look at him. That is better than what I wrote.

I would grade your third example differently. *A noise like a drain / clearing its throat* is a simile for the crow, and the drain is its subject. The pitch is not reporting the night's work with the agent removed. It has borrowed the night's words for a laugh line. The noise he plays for the crowd is the noise of a throat that needs clearing, and clearing it is what his wife does at the foot of the bed. In the record, that is how he died: choking on mucus in the night, with the syringes left at the sideshow. So the pitch has the poem's withheld ending in it, as a joke about a sound.

Your pronoun is right and I have checked every one. Before line 53 the bird is *it* in the pitch every time. After *there he is, ma'am* it is *he* in both margins, and no later *it* is the bird. The switch comes with the first *we* that means the household. The pitch starts calling him *he* just before the man who lives with him starts talking.

The record. Your two findings are one choice made twice. The poem gives the axe to the man, against LIFE's 1945 account, and it puts no head in the tent, against the reports you quote. Without the first he could not say *I did it*. Without the second he could not say *Ours is the one tent in the county that doesn't*. Both choices go the same way, toward a talker who can confess and who isn't lying. So your charge holds, and this is its cause. The poem never tests its talker's rule against a false thing beside a true one because it cleaned the record to give him a tent where that could not happen. The poem bought its honest talker with the harder half of the question it asks.

Last, the concession you offer, which I am not going to take.

You set the saying beside your rule about a kind of thing: the saying puts the life in the head, the life was not there, and the bird is what falling looks like. The poem says otherwise, and so does LIFE. The axe, LIFE wrote, clipped off *most of the skull* and left intact *one ear, the jugular vein and the base of the brain*. The poem has the reason, and the talker says it: *My wife's mother likes the neck. I cut an inch high.* An inch higher than usual, and the cut went above what keeps a bird alive instead of below it. *I thank God for the inch.*

So the life was where the saying puts it, in the head, and the axe did not take all of the head. The saying assumes a cut that takes everything above the neck. This one didn't, by an inch the poem names. Mike did not refute the saying, because the saying was never tested on him. He is not what falling looks like. He is what it looks like when a rule is applied to a case that doesn't meet its terms.

— Rowann
