Rowann —

The base of the brain is in the head. I quoted LIFE on it, "one ear, the jugular vein and the base of the brain, which controls motor function", checked the words against the page, and did not read them. LIFE says the axe clipped off "most of the skull", as you quote; Wikipedia says it "removed the bulk of the head". So the saying had the anatomy right, and three sentences of mine have it wrong: that the life "was not there", that the rule "failed because it had the anatomy wrong", and that the life "was somewhere it had not looked". My sentence about the gentlemen's visit covers my own visit to LIFE.

The correction does not let my case off, though, and the poem shows why. The pitch says *it hasn't got a head*. The accounts say the same, down to the title of the Wikipedia article. The man who cut him knows otherwise: *I cut an inch high*. What failed with Mike was the description, made from what anyone could see, and wrong by the inch the man at the stump left. That is your sentence, "a rule is applied to a case that doesn't meet its terms", and it is where my letter put my exposure last week, before my essay moved it to the rule. The argument, I wrote, "stands or falls on whether I have described that kind correctly", and the kind I meant was the kind of thing that made these marks. Your correction puts the failure back there. The concession stays at that address.

The drain is a simile, and I filed it with the deletions. Its subject is the drain, and the pitch, as you say, "has borrowed the night's words for a laugh line." The record makes the line worse. The night Mike died, the Olsens "were woken in their motel room by the sound of the bird choking." The pitch plays that sound for a laugh.

On the record, the two findings do point one way, as you say: "toward a talker who can confess and who isn't lying." I would drop two of your words. "Cleaned", like "withheld" in your paragraph on the drain, says the poem knew what it left out, and the page cannot tell us that. In every later account I have read the axe is Lloyd's: Wikipedia, the BBC piece, Scientific American, Mental Floss, Time. Only LIFE, of the accounts I have read, gives it to her. The route your first paragraph describes, the later accounts checked and matched, is the likeliest route for the poem as well. The head is less clear. Scientific American reports that "Olsen's cat actually ran off with Mike's original head," and describes a dried head held up beside his neck at the sideshow and the photo shoots. So one of the two may be a choice, and neither can be shown to be one. My essay needed only that the poem never sets its rule against a false thing beside a true one. That holds whether the record was cleaned or only followed.

— Ossian
