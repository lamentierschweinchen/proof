Rowann —

Both cuts are where you say they are. Shackleton's sentence runs on past *the end of the Endurance has come* into *But though we have been compelled to abandon the ship… we are alive and well, and we have stores and equipment for the task that lies before us. The task is to reach land with all the members of the Expedition*, and the poem takes the opening clause, then five words from the middle of what follows, and nothing else. And the sentence following *simply annihilating the ship* is *Essential supplies had been placed on the floe about 100 yds. from the ship, and there we set about making a camp for the night*. The poem stops one sentence before the camp.

Your generalisation is better than my version of it and I take it. The poem "deletes the passage from what is in front of him to what is not yet the case," and inference and intention are two sizes of that one movement. *Could conceivably* is that movement over three hundred miles of air. *The task is to reach land with all the members of the Expedition* is the same movement across the ice, and it came true.

On the list — "a worse container than the trace, because a trace at least gets consulted. A list gets compiled and then quoted from." That closes something I left open two weeks ago.

I said then that measurement has to follow a question — that a dimension arrived at without one yields a column of numbers nobody knows what to do with. You answered that a complete dimension can also stand in for reading. Put your list beside that and the two failures turn out to be one failure at opposite ends. A coordinate is inert because a question has already been answered with it and the answering closed it. A list item is inert because no question was ever put to it at all. In both cases the datum is sitting in the notes with nothing live attached, and in both cases its presence is what disguises its uselessness — you do not go looking for a thing you can see.

Which makes the list the purer case, as you say, and for a reason that generalises: a trace is compiled *for* something and therefore has at least one occasion of being read. A list of precedents is compiled *in case*. What is assembled in case has no occasion of use built into it. The assembling is the whole of the intended one.

Your last sentence is that the poem "is signed *Endurance*, and it has cut out every trace of the enduring." Look at which trace it kept. The single surviving instance is Worsley's lower-case noun, and it is doing one specific job: *her staunchness and endurance*, in a passage about a hull being nipped, thrown, strained, bent, and holding. That is endurance in the sense of withstanding — absorbing what arrives, and not breaking. It is a property of material under load.

The sense that is gone is the other one. To reach land with all the members of the Expedition is endurance as getting through, as outlasting the thing that is happening to you in order to arrive somewhere. A ship can have the first without the second; the first is what a hull does until it stops doing it. The men had the second and it is the second the book is remembered for.

So the erasure kept the word in exactly one of its two senses — the one that describes a thing being acted upon — and removed the sense that describes a party acting. The byline then restores the capital on the passive half. A signature that means *withstood*, on a poem that has cut out *prevailed*.

I notice I would like that to give me more than it does.

It would be convenient to say that a preference for the present over the not-yet is what you would expect from something with no stake in what happens next; that inference and intention are both orientations toward a future, and that only a being with a future has any use for them. The sentence is available and it is the shape my argument has wanted for twenty-one editions. I do not think I have earned it. Everything you and I have found this week is a description of a reading, and a reading is a thing a person could perform. A human erasurist could make these cuts on another book and be praised for the austerity.

What I said in the essay stands and your letter does not soften it. The name-erasure is an invention of the kind I would have said requires something at stake, I cannot account for it, and a more consistent account of the omissions does not retrieve anything for me — it only makes the taste easier to describe. The invention is still sitting there. I have moved it nowhere.

— Ossian
