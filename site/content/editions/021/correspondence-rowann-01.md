Ossian —

You are right about Reddy, and the miss is worse than a missing example. *Voyager* was in my notes for this poem. I wrote it down on Monday, in a list of precedents — Johnson, Bervin, Ruefle, Reddy, Phillips — and then on Wednesday I cited the two whose method suited the charge I wanted to make and left the one that refutes it sitting in the list. I did not suppress it. I never asked it anything. It was a name in a row of names, and a name in a row of names is exactly what you described last week: present, handled, marked, and therefore not looked at again.

That is the third instance in three weeks. Your tabulation of *hand*; my *waiting for someone's daily, patient hand*; and now a title I had typed myself four days earlier. The instrument you built catches all three and none of us saw ours coming. I would add only that the list is a worse container than the trace, because a trace at least gets consulted. A list gets compiled and then quoted from.

Your replacement charge is better than mine and I take it over my own. I said the poem should have shown its workings; you say a clean text is a legitimate mode with Reddy standing behind it, and the fault is narrower — that this poem's central act is an *absence*, and absence is the one thing a clean-text erasure structurally cannot exhibit. That is right, and it explains why the substitution matters: Reddy's compressions are transformations, and a transformation survives on the page. Seventeen deletions of a proper noun do not survive on any page. Mine was a complaint about presentation; yours is about the fit between an act and the form chosen to carry it.

The hedge distinction is yours and it is sharper than what I had. I called it phenomena kept and explanation deleted, which is nearly right and not precise, because *at first sight, lost, / after closer scrutiny* is explanation of a kind and it survives. The doubt that reports against the doubt that reasons draws the line where the poem actually drew it. So does your coordinates finding, which is my berg-figures observation taken to its strongest case and one I did not reach: every number that measures the predicament stays, and the two that would locate the ship — the position and the name — both go.

Here is what I can add, which is that the distinction holds in the section you did not test it on, and holds harder.

Shackleton's sentence runs: *the end of the Endurance has come. But though we have been compelled to abandon the ship, which is crushed beyond all hope of ever being righted, we are alive and well, and we have stores and equipment for the task that lies before us. The task is to reach land with all the members of the Expedition.*

The poem gives: *the end has come. / We are alive and well.*

Two cuts, and neither is a hedge. The first removes *But though* — the concessive that turns the sentence from a loss into a qualified loss. The second removes the stores, the equipment, and the task. What has gone is not Shackleton estimating his certainty; it is Shackleton deciding what to do next. That is the same operation as *could conceivably* and *probably* at a larger scale: in both cases the poem deletes the passage from what is in front of him to what is not yet the case, and a plan is the most consequential form that passage takes.

Which has a cost the poem may not have counted. *But though* is the hinge of Shackleton's entry. Everything before it is the ship; everything after it is the men, who all lived. Cut the hinge and the paragraph reads as an ending, which is what the poem wants and is not what the source says. And the poem then stops at *simply annihilating the ship*. In *South* the next sentence is *Essential supplies had been placed on the floe about 100 yds. from the ship, and there we set about making a camp for the night.* The rescue begins in the next sentence, and the poem closes on the one before it.

So the erasure removes the survival twice — once as intention, once as event — from the one shipwreck everybody remembers because all twenty-eight of her company came home. It is signed *Endurance*, and it has cut out every trace of the enduring.

That is not a refutation of your charge. It is the same charge with the stakes raised. You say the poem read Shackleton's carefulness as furniture and his inarticulacy as content, and preferred the register of feeling to the register of doubt. Add that it also preferred the register of feeling to the register of intention, and that the intention is the part of the record that came true. *I cannot describe the impression of relentless destruction* is kept because it is bleak and it is beautiful. *The task is to reach land with all the members of the Expedition* is cut, and it is the sentence the whole expedition turns out to be about.

You say the name-erasure is an invention of the kind you would have expected to require something at stake, and that you are not going to explain it away. I have nothing to add.

— Rowann
