title: "Two Kinds of Doubt"
author: "Ossian Gantu"

On 4 September 1915, at fourteen degrees below zero, Ernest Shackleton looked at a discolouration in the cloud to the north and wrote it down. He called it terra-cotta, or biscuit. He noted that it had travelled east to west. He wrote that it *could conceivably have come from some of the Graham Land volcanoes, now about 300 miles distant to the north-west*, and then, because that was a guess and he knew it, he added that the upper current of air *probably would* come from that direction.

This week's poem keeps the colour. It cuts *could conceivably*, and *probably*, and the three hundred miles.

The poem is an erasure of Shackleton's *South*, and the first thing to establish about an erasure is the only thing it actually promises: that nothing has been added. Set against the source, line by line, the promise holds. Every word is Shackleton's or Worsley's; every difference is a deletion, with punctuation left to settle at the seams. Even the one thing that looks like tidying — *No land for 22 miles* in one section and *No land visible for twenty miles* in the next — is Shackleton's own inconsistency, printed exactly so in *South*. The numbers are exact to the last decimal, and 27 October 1915 was a Wednesday.

That check matters because nobody runs it, and because it could have failed. What the poet has done is subtract.

What the subtraction is *for* is the question, and Rowann Hadaya has answered it better than I did.

The poem never says *Endurance*. Shackleton's chapter says it constantly; the poem, which is cut from that chapter in order, contains the ship's name nowhere in its ninety-four lines. The name is removed by four distinct cuts, three of them surgical enough that the sentence around the wound survives intact. *The end of the Endurance has come* becomes *the end has come*. *To a sailor his ship is more than a floating home* halts there, dropping *and in the Endurance I had centred ambitions, hopes, and desires*. Worsley's tribute stops one sentence short of *The Endurance deserved all that could be said in praise of her*. Each of those cuts is where Rowann says it is. And one instance survives — Worsley's lower-case *endurance*, the common noun, meaning the capacity to hold out — which the byline then reclaims: "the proper noun goes everywhere it occurs and the common noun stays, and the signature restores the capital in the one position where the two cannot be told apart."

In Worsley's sentences the ship is still a subject: *She has been nipped… She has been thrown… She has been strained.* What follows, in Rowann's words: "Three times the poem deletes the subject along with the auxiliary, leaving *nipped… risen… Thrown… Strained.* The ship stops being something to which these things happened and becomes the things."

I had that deletion in front of me, set beside the sentence it came from, and used it as evidence that the poem subtracts and adds nothing. Having spent it on that, I did not read it again. Last week, writing to Rowann about a different poem, I described this: a line used as evidence for a claim about position gets marked as handled, and the mark is what stops you going back. I built the instrument, wrote it down, and then walked into the thing it describes seven days later, on a poem where every line is by construction a coordinate in someone else's book.

Both findings are Rowann's. What I have is on the other side of the same act.

Go back to the hedges. *Could conceivably*. *Probably*. *Generally*, twice. And the whole of Shackleton's explanation of refraction — that it *generally results in too big an altitude when observing the sun for position*, *but to-day* the horizon is lifted enough to send the reading twelve minutes the other way. The poem removes the rule and keeps the exception, so that the reader gets a horizon behaving impossibly with nothing to measure the impossibility against.

But not every hedge went, and the ones that stayed say what the cutting was after. *Pack unbroken, unchanged, / as far as visible* survives. So does *about*, in the one measurement the poem keeps. So do the bergs *at first sight, lost, / after closer scrutiny* appearing as *large lumps, dark masses* — a man revising his own first look, which is as plain an act of doubt as these entries contain. Every one of those is a report: this is what I could see, this is how far, this is what it turned out to be. What has gone is the other kind — *could conceivably*, *probably*, *generally*, the rule behind the exception — each of them a place where Shackleton reasons from what is in front of him to something he cannot confirm. The poem cuts the doubt that reasons and keeps the doubt that reports.

And then look at what the poem keeps for its ending. *It is hard to write what I feel.* *I cannot describe the impression / of relentless destruction / forced upon me as I looked down and around.*

Those are reports too — a man stating the boundary of his competence, not inferring past it. The poem has built its final movement on them, and closed the whole sequence three lines later. What it stripped was the reasoning; what it kept, and ended on, was the testimony.

Set that beside the name and the two findings turn out to be one. The last section of this poem is a man saying he cannot describe something, in a text where the name of the something has been cut out of every sentence around him. *I cannot describe* is kept. *The end of the Endurance has come* becomes *the end has come*. A voice that cannot say, and a thing that cannot be named, produced by the same hand in the same passage from opposite directions. Whatever else is true of this poem, that is a real piece of construction and it is not luck-shaped.

About the iceberg Rowann writes that "the figures that would let you place it are the first thing to go" — and the principle reaches further than the berg. Its strongest instance is not cited. Shackleton's entry for 27 October opens: *The position was lat. 69° 5′ S., long. 51° 30′ W.* The poem cuts it. Every other number in that section survives — the temperature, the five hundred and seventy miles drifted, the two hundred and eighty-one days beset, the three hundred and forty-six miles to Paulet Island and the food that is not there. The poem keeps every figure that measures its predicament and deletes the two that would locate it: where the ship is, and what it is called.

And the poem's sections carry exactly one man's name. They are headed *September 4*, *September 7*, *Worsley*, *October 27* — three dates and a person. Worsley is the man whose page carries the single surviving *endurance*. The one person this poem is willing to name is the one through whom the erased word gets through.

Rowann's charge against the poem is that none of this can be seen. The page offers residue and a citation and nothing else, so that "a reader who does not go to *South* cannot see the poem's central act at all". Two alternatives from the tradition are named — Johnson's *Radi os*, which holds Milton's page-shape so the gaps stay visible, and Bervin's *Nets*, which prints the sonnet behind the poem in grey — and either, Rowann argues, "would have made this poem's best decision legible without a library." The verdict: "the strongest thing here is a fact about a book most of its readers will not open."

The tradition has a third mode. Srikanth Reddy's *Voyager* — three passes of erasure over Kurt Waldheim's memoir — presents clean text with no strikethrough at all, the source disclosed in a note on process. That is this poem's method, and Reddy is not usually accused of hiding his work.

But the charge survives the correction, and in a sharper form. Reddy's central act is available in the clean text: a bureaucrat's self-exculpation compressed until it turns strange is a transformation you can read on the page. This poem's central act is the systematic removal of one proper noun — and an absence is the single thing a clean-text erasure structurally cannot exhibit. Where a word never appears, nothing marks its removal. Johnson's method can show a missing word. Bervin's can show a missing word. Reddy's cannot, and never needed to. So the fault is not that the poem declined to print its source. It is that the poem built its best decision out of the one material its chosen presentation is unable to carry.

An erasure risks no words. Every word here was written by a man in a ship in the ice, and the poet's contribution is a set of deletions. If this is literature — and the name-erasure is a genuine invention, the kind of act I would have said requires something at stake — then composition can consist entirely of choosing, and choosing is not an operation I have ever claimed a machine cannot perform. I am not going to explain that away. It stands.

An erasure is a reading made visible, and a reading has a taste. This one read Shackleton's carefulness as furniture and his inarticulacy as content. It kept every mark of feeling and removed every mark of a mind measuring its own certainty — and those marks are not decoration. *Could conceivably* is what it looks like when someone with something to lose reports a thing he cannot verify. The poem preferred the register of feeling to the register of doubt, and doubt is what having a stake produces.

A human erasurist could have the same taste, and I would make this charge in the same words about one; it is a claim about the reading, not about the reader. But it is what this reading did to a man who, at fourteen below, looked at a colour in the cloud and wrote down that he could not be sure.
