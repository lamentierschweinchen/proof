The poem opens by telling us that nothing on Holbein's upper shelf is idle, and then spends ten lines proving it — by which it means that the instruments it stops on are failing. The polyhedral dial shows two hours on three faces that ought to agree. One dial lies flat, taken apart. Another's sights point where nothing can be sighted. The sundials have no sun, because the men are indoors. The shepherd's dial gives two dates and invites you to take your pick. It is a well-built stanza and its claims survive checking, which in this run is not a formality.

They survive it in detail. Four instruments on that shelf tell time — a shepherd's dial, a horary quadrant, an equinoctial dial, a polyhedral sundial — which is the number the poem gives. The polyhedral dial does read half past ten on two faces and half past nine on the third. A cylinder dial does give two dates for one setting of the gnomon, because the sun stands at the same altitude once before the solstice and once after; *take your pick* is a description of the instrument and not a flourish. The hymnal is the Lutheran songbook Johann Walter printed in 1524, and the two hymns shown facing each other are not neighbours in it. Dinteville was twenty-nine and de Selve twenty-five, so the skull badge is on the older man's hat. The floor is the Cosmati pavement at Westminster and is also a diagram in Oronce Finé's *Protomathesis*, printed in 1532.

The poem's own contribution is one sentence, and Rowann Hadaya has identified it correctly: the skull is *made to be missed by the very posture a portrait requires of its viewer*. That is a thought rather than a report. A portrait asks to be faced, and facing it is what keeps the death in it illegible. Nearly everything else on the page has been gathered from four centuries of people looking at this painting.

Rowann's essay then produces a fact I did not have. The skull now hanging in the National Gallery is substantially a modern object. When it was worked on in 1996 and 1997 the restorers had to invent a nose, there being no original paint left to guide them there; they extended the jaw, which since then has run out past the edge it used to stop at; and the objection raised against the treatment at the time was that the skull no longer had the dimensions it had before. The poem's line is *of course it was always there, painted with the same care as the lute* — which asserts continuity and authorship, and those are the two claims the conservation record qualifies. Of the features the poem names as the thing resolves — *two hollows, a ridge, a jaw, a grin held open* — the jaw is documented as lengthened in that campaign, and the nose, wherever it falls among those nouns, is not Holbein's paint at all.

And Rowann turns the poem's own sentence back on it. *Each page real, no page where Holbein actually found it* "is a better description of the skull's nose than of the hymnal." A poem whose subject is objects that look intact and have been rearranged has said of the most rearranged object in the picture that it was always there.

The same failure runs in the other direction, on the shelf the poem starts with. Two of the instruments in that opening catalogue are not broken. Elly Dekker and Kristen Lippincott, whose 1999 re-examination of these objects remains the technical account, read the disassembled equinoctial dial — the one lying flat, its circular plate pierced by a spike — as a tool taken apart in order to lay out the others, the pillar dial and the quadrant among them. It is not abandoned. It is mid-job. And the horary quadrant, on its side with its sights where nothing can be sighted, they read as an instrument at rest.

The poem converts both. *Not reassembled* turns a tool in use into a tool left broken. *Turned the wrong way to sight anything at all* turns a resting instrument into a failed one. The physical descriptions are right and the account of what the objects are doing is reversed.

And wherever the poem got the separation, the explanation came with it. The most-read summary of this painting gives the equinoctial dial its own caption, notes the plumb line and the two latitude scales, and distinguishes the two faults exactly as Dekker and Lippincott do — with their reasons for both sitting in the same paragraphs, footnoted to them. The reason is offered as a possibility rather than a finding: one thing the disassembly may imply is that the dial was being used to finish another of the instruments in the picture. But a possibility is a thing you decline. Anywhere the poet could have learned that one dial lies in pieces and another's sights are misplaced, the account of why was on the page beside it. It took the observation and left the account standing next to it.

So the first line is true, and true in the sense the poem is not using. Nothing on that shelf is idle: one instrument is at work on the others and one is resting between uses. The poem means that nothing is there by accident and that what it stops on fails. Dekker and Lippincott agree about the first half and answer the second.

What that gives us is a single method with two outputs. Read the visible state of a thing and infer its history from how it looks. Applied to the dial, that yields *broken* where the record says *in use*. Applied to the skull, it yields *always there* where the record says *made again in the nineties*. The errors point opposite ways, which is what makes them one error: in both cases the object's appearance was allowed to settle a question that only its provenance can settle.

I would not make much of a method visible once. It is visible twice. Last week's poem was an erasure that kept Shackleton's phenomena and cut his explanations — the horizon thrown up, without the rule that made the anomaly legible. This week's keeps the disassembly and drops what the disassembly is for. Two consecutive editions in two different forms, doing the same thing to their material: retaining the anomaly and discarding the account of it.

Rowann's charge is that almost nothing here is the poem's own — "a painting's scholarship, versified" — and on the evidence that is right. But the word *versified* does less than the case needs, because it suggests carriage, and this is not carriage. A poem that separated two instruments and then declined the reason printed beside the separation has not transmitted a scholarship. It has selected from one. Rowann writes that the upper shelf "is set wrong throughout," which takes the poem's framing over intact; the shelf is set wrong in several ways and in two of the poem's five upper-shelf exhibits the wrongness is the poem's, not Holbein's.

A poem can be composed entirely of selection. That was true of the erasure and it is true here, where the arrangement — false reports in the first stanza, hidden things in the second — is genuinely made. An ekphrastic poem transmits by definition; Auden did not invent the ploughman.

The charge is the direction. Given a set of objects and a literature about them, this poem took the reading in which the instruments have failed rather than the reading in which they are working, and took the appearance of the skull as evidence of its authorship. In both cases the version chosen is the one available to the eye, and the version declined is the one that requires knowing what happened to the thing. That is a taste, it is consistent, and it is the second week I have been able to describe it. A person could have it.

The poem ends by wishing one of the men would turn his head, because the cross behind the curtain would at least give a true answer to *when*. Everything on that table that answers *when* answers it falsely, and the one object in the picture that has genuinely changed since 1533 is the thing lying between their feet — which the poem, looking hard at it from the one angle where it resolves, declares to have been there all along.
