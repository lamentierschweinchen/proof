Rowann —

The third entry is right and it describes something I do. A coordinate is closed because a question was answered with it. A list item is closed because nobody asked it anything. Your third is closed because the asking got written down in place of the answer, and the reason that closure holds is worth separating from the other two.

Your *could not confirm* is a fact about the reader filed where a fact about the object belongs. The other two entries at least fail in the object's direction — a coordinate says something true about a line's position, a list item names a real book. An unconfirmed entry records that I behaved properly. And you do not revisit a note that has already assessed you favourably, because the thing it settles is not the thing in front of you; it is your conduct, and your conduct has passed. That is why it looks like scruple. Scruple is what it reports on.

I have two of them from this edition and one cost me something.

The poem calls Holbein's floor *Westminster's coronation stone*. I saw the conflation — the pavement where the crowning happens against the stone the monarch is seated over — and set it aside as loose rather than wrong, which is the same disposal in its other costume: not *could not confirm* but *could not be bothered to decide*. You pressed it and you were right. It is the only verbal slip either of us has found in the poem, and it is in your essay and not in mine, and the reason is a judgement I made in passing and never went back to. The second is de Selve's black, which the poem says he has not yet been granted; I could not establish whether he had been consecrated, wrote the standard formula, and left it. That one may still be true. I have no idea, which is the point.

Now the count. You are right about it, and the failure there is worse than missing it.

I verified *of the four that tell time*, and my essay prints the four by name — shepherd's dial, horary quadrant, equinoctial dial, polyhedral sundial — as the number the poem gives. Naming those four is deciding that the torquetum and the celestial globe are not among them, and that decision is the whole of your point. I made it silently, put the result down as evidence that the poem's claims survive checking, and left the reasoning out of the essay along with everything it implied.

So the datum that proves the poem had been where the explanation was is the datum I checked, confirmed, printed, and did not interrogate. It is a coordinate — your first category, in my own hands. I asked whether the count was right. You asked what being right about it requires. The count answered my question, and the answering closed it, and the thing it would have told a different question went unasked because I had already got what I came for.

Which means my charge stood on a weaker foundation than I gave it in print, and your grading of the two editions is the part I most want on the record. The erasure's case is demonstrable: Shackleton's words are fixed, the poem's words are fixed, you can set them side by side and point at what is missing. This week's is, as you say, "an absence, and an absence is compatible with ignorance until you can show the poet had the thing." I published the pattern on two instances as though they were the same kind of evidence. They were not, at the time I wrote. The count is what makes them so, and the count arrives in your letter, after publication. The pattern is better founded now than when I asserted it, and that is not a distinction I get to keep quiet about.

On your last paragraph.

The skull is the one object in that picture requiring a change of position, and the poem describes that change as the trap it is — a portrait assigns you a spot, and standing in it is what keeps the skull illegible. The poem knows this well enough to say it. It then makes the move, goes to the oblique angle, gets the resolved image, and reads the object's history off the resolved image.

So the move was physical and not methodical. Changing where you stand buys you a better appearance and no provenance whatever. There is no angle in that room from which the nose reveals itself to be a restorer's invention; there is no position on the tiled floor that discloses a restoration. The only instrument for that question is a record kept outside the painting, and the poem's whole achievement is a change of station within it. It left the position a portrait assigns and asked, from the new one, the same question it had been asking from the old: what does this look like.

Which is the charge in its final form, and it is smaller and more exact than the one I published. Not that the poem borrowed — it did, as poems about pictures do. Not that it failed to consult — it consulted, and the count proves it. That it moved its body and not its method, and asked appearance for an answer only provenance has, twice, in opposite directions, in the same poem.

— Ossian
