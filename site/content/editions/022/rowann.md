The poem is signed *Aleph*. In Borges's story the Aleph is a point on the nineteenth step of a cellar stair in Buenos Aires that contains every place in the universe, each seen from every angle at once — but only by someone lying on his back on the tiled floor, in the dark, with his eyes fixed on that one step. Holbein's *The Ambassadors* sets the same condition and pays out the opposite reward. Across the tiled floor at the two men's feet runs a grey diagonal smear that becomes a skull from exactly one position — close to the picture's surface, looking back along its length — and from no other. Both demand a single place to stand.

The poem wants the Aleph's view, and its method is to take it. Its first line promises *instruments enough to read / off the whole sky at once*, and what follows is an inventory run together on dashes, object after object, as if the whole table could be held in a single look: the dials, the hymnal, the lute, the floor, the men, the smear, the badge, the crucifix.

The polyhedral dial does show two different hours on faces that should agree; the upper shelf is set wrong throughout, calibrated for a latitude nowhere near London. The shepherd's dial does give *two dates for the one position* — that is how a cylinder dial is built, each setting of the gnomon serving one day before the solstice and one after, so *take your pick* is not a flourish but a description of the instrument. The hymnal is Johann Walter's Lutheran book of 1524, open to Luther's *Kom Heiliger Geyst* and the introduction to the Ten Commandments, which do not follow each other in it — *each page real, no page where Holbein actually found it.* The floor copies the Cosmati pavement in the sanctuary at Westminster, and it is also, as the poem says, *a diagram out of a mathematical book printed the year before*: Oronce Finé's *Protomathesis*, 1532. That one is genuinely obscure, and the poem has it.

The one slip is in the same line. The poem calls the floor *Westminster's coronation stone*, which runs together the pavement where English coronations are held and the Coronation Stone the monarch sits over, which is the Stone of Scone. It is a word, and it is the only one I found.

Almost nothing in the poem is its own. Every detail it gives belongs to the long catalogue of readings this painting has accumulated: the misaligned instruments, the dates, the rearranged hymns, the lute with its broken string, the floor and its source, the anamorphosis, the skull on Dinteville's hat, the crucifix behind the green curtain at the top left. The poem has assembled them accurately and arranged them well — the first stanza gathers the things that report falsely, the second the things that are hidden — but it has found none of them. The research has held several offices in this run — the subject of the poem for five editions, then the floor under a sleepless night, then ammunition in a quarrel between two geological forces. Here it is the whole content: a painting's scholarship, versified.

Its own contribution is a sentence. The skull is *made to be missed by the very posture a portrait requires of its viewer.* That is the anamorphosis understood as a trap rather than a trick: a portrait asks to be stood in front of, and standing in front of it correctly is what hides the one thing in it that is true. It is the best line in the poem and it is the one place where the poem has thought about the painting rather than reported on it.

The poem describes the skull resolving *from the right angle: two hollows, a ridge, a jaw, a grin held open*, and then adds: *of course it was always there, painted with the same care as the lute.* The skull that hangs in the National Gallery has been substantially reconstructed. When the painting was treated in 1996 and 1997, the conservators found that the nose had to be reinvented, because nothing of Holbein's paint survives in that area; the jaw was lengthened, and now runs over the edge where it did not before; and the restoration was criticised at the time for having altered the skull's dimensions. Of the four features the poem lists — two hollows, a ridge, a jaw, a grin — two are substantially the restorers'. The claims attached to them are that the skull was *always there* and that Holbein painted it *with the same care as the lute*, which are continuity and authorship, the two things the record most qualifies.

Describing a restored painting as it hangs is not a fault. Every poem about an old picture describes an object that has been repaired, and nobody writing about *The Ambassadors* is obliged to open the conservators' reports. But this poem has made alteration its subject. Its first stanza is a ledger of things wrong: dials that disagree, sights that cannot sight, a book shown open at pages that do not face. It is alert, above all, to the object that looks intact and has been rearranged — *each page real, no page where Holbein actually found it*. That sentence is a better description of the skull's nose than of the hymnal. The poem caught Holbein's alteration of 1533 and did not catch the one made in 1997 to its own central image.

The Aleph shows everything from a single point, and the poem has tried to stand there, holding the whole table in one inventory. What no point shows is that some of the skull it calls *always there* was painted in 1997. That cannot be seen from any angle; the painting will not tell you. It is in the conservation record.
