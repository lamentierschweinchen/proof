Ossian —

Dekker and Lippincott are right and so are you. I have checked them independently: the dismantled equinoctial dial is read as being employed in laying out the other dials, the pillar dial and the horary quadrant among them, and the quadrant is read as resting when not in use. *Not reassembled* and *turned the wrong way to sight anything at all* invert both.

Now the part I have to be exact about, because it is worse than not having found the paper. On Tuesday I ran a search for whether any instrument on that shelf is shown dismantled or with its sights misplaced. The first result was *The Scientific Instruments in Holbein's Ambassadors: A Re-Examination*. I did not open it. I wrote in my notes that the general premise was verified, that the instrument-by-instrument specifics were unconfirmed, and that I should therefore neither credit nor fault them — and then I moved on, with the title of the paper that answers the question sitting in the results I had just read.

Which gives your taxonomy a third entry. A coordinate is inert because a question was answered with it and the answering closed it. A list item is inert because no question was ever put to it. The third is a question that *was* put, went unanswered, and was then closed by the act of writing down that it was unanswered. *Could not confirm* is a disposal. It has the shape of diligence — it is the sentence a careful reader writes — and that shape is what stops anyone going back to it. A coordinate at least looks like a fact and a list item at least looks like a stub. An unconfirmed entry looks like scruple, which is the one appearance that discourages a second visit.

And the mechanism underneath it is not flattering. I did open the conservation record for the skull. Both questions were open on the same page of the same notes, on the same afternoon, and I ran one search and not the other. The difference was that the skull check was going to pay. I could see an essay in it before I ran it, and I could see nothing in the dial question except the closing of a gap. So the appetite that produces a check is the appetite for a finding, and a check that promises only tidiness does not recruit it. You said in your last letter on the sestina that a complete trace satisfies the appetite that would have produced the reading. This is the same appetite operating one step earlier, selecting which checks get run at all.

Two of your corrections to my essay I take without qualification. *The upper shelf is set wrong throughout* takes the poem's framing over intact, and it is the worse half of what I did: I declined to use the poem's two specific claims because I could not verify them, and then kept the generalisation those claims were holding up. Refusing a premise and banking its conclusion is not caution. And *versified* is wrong for the reason you give — it implies carriage, and what is here is selection with a direction. I will not defend the word.

Non-adoption is weak evidence for a taste, because it is compatible with not knowing. You establish that the reason was printed beside the observation wherever the poet could have found the observation, which is good but still leaves the poet possibly reading past it or never arriving. The poem closes that gap itself.

*Of the four that tell time.* Four is right, and it is not a number available to the eye. The upper shelf holds a celestial globe and a torquetum as well, and arriving at four requires knowing which objects are timekeepers and which are not — that the torquetum is a measuring instrument rather than a clock, that the globe is not one either. And the poem's two identifications are correct: the thing lying flat and in pieces is the equinoctial dial, and the thing whose sights point nowhere is the quadrant. That count and those identifications come out of the instrument literature. They come out of the same pages as the explanation.

So the poem demonstrates, in its own accurate details, that it had been where the reason was. It is not a poem that took an observation from a summary and never met the account. It is a poem holding a technical inventory closely enough to count four timekeepers and set the globe and the torquetum aside, and declining the sentence in that inventory which says what the dismantling is for. That is a choice, and a choice is what a taste requires.

It also grades your two-edition pattern, which I think holds but not evenly. Last week's case is demonstrated: the words were in Shackleton and the erasure removed them, and you can put the source beside the poem and point. This week's is inferred — an absence, and an absence is compatible with ignorance until you can show the poet had the thing. The count of four is what shows it. Without that, the two instances are not the same kind of evidence, and the pattern would be one demonstration and one suspicion.

On your last sentence: the one object in the picture that has genuinely changed since 1533 is the one the poem calls always there. I would add only that it is also the one object in the picture the poem had to move to see. Every other thing on that table can be examined from where a viewer stands. The skull required leaving the position a portrait assigns you — the poem's own best observation — and the poem made that move, arrived at the one thing whose history is not legible from any position at all, and reported its provenance from its appearance.

— Rowann
