title: "The Air It Left"
author: "Rowann Hadaya"

The people who raised the first class of whooping cranes to be led south behind an aircraft wore white smocks with hoods and goggles, and fed the chicks with hand puppets made to look like adult cranes. Nobody was allowed to talk near them. While the birds were still in the egg they were played recordings of an ultralight engine, and in the autumn they followed one from Wisconsin to Florida, with a crane's brood call coming from a loudspeaker on its landing gear. That was 2001. The aircraft led a class south every year until 2015, and in January 2016 the Fish and Wildlife Service said it would stop.

"Class of 2001" is spoken by that first class, and its first stanza is the costume seen from below: *What fed us was a head on a white arm. / Above the arm, a bag. In the bag, a window. / Behind the window, someone. We did not know someone.* Each short sentence goes one layer further in, from the puppet to the smock to the eyepiece, and at the last layer there is *someone*, whom they *did not know*. The poem gives places and months their ordinary names, *the runway*, *Florida*, *October*, and it names the bobcats. It names nobody in the program. The aircraft is only an engine, and the word comes four times; there is no plane in the poem and no pilot. The people are *no one*, *someone* and *they*. The word *crane* appears once, and it is the recording: *a crane on a tape calling us on*. The speakers never name their own kind, and the word *bird* comes only in the last line, inside the sentence that begins *They said*.

Seven lines are indented, and they are the seven in which the birds move or look along the engine's course: running behind it down the runway, going *after it, eight*, riding off its wingtip, and in April looking north *where the engine / had once been going*. The engine's own line stays at the margin, *In October it went up ahead of us, calling*, and so do the waiting and the dying. The longest sentence in the poem, thirty-four words over four indented lines, is the one in which the birds do least: *When it could, we went after it, eight, / the first of us six inches off the wingtip, / held up by the air it left rising behind it, / and hardly had to beat a wing.*

The air is right. A wing sheds a vortex from each tip, the air just outboard of it rises, and the Smithsonian's account has the cranes jockeying for a position "to 'surf' the strong wing tip vortex generated by the ultralight". The poem is signed *Wake*, the word for that air. The word is not in the poem, and that makes it the first signature since Edition 22 that the text does not contain.

The count comes in the poem's shortest sentences, three words each: *That was seven. Seven reached Florida. / Bobcats took two.* Each number has a bird behind it in the International Crane Foundation's records. In an October storm a pen blew down, and the bird that got out "hit a power line while flying in the dark". Journey North's table for the year has eight birds at takeoff, seven reaching Florida, and twenty-three days grounded out of forty-eight, which is *Half the days it could not*. One of the seven had been barred from flying for dropping out of formation and finished the trip in a shipping container. He was killed by a bobcat on 17 December, a few days after arriving, and another bird was killed in January. Nothing led them north in the spring. Four of the five left on 9 April and reached Wisconsin on the 19th, and the fifth came in on 3 May. *Five of us looked north, where the engine / had once been going. The front of the air was empty. / We went into it.* The last of those lines is back at the margin. The only other *into* in the poem is in the first stanza: *We hatched into a room where no one spoke.*

The last stanza's figures are right as well. In January 2016 the Associated Press reported that nearly 250 cranes had been released in Wisconsin since 2001, and Fish and Wildlife officials said that about 93 were alive and that "only 10 chicks have survived to fledge". What changes is who is speaking. Until now the voice has been made by leaving things out: the records know the aircraft, the pilots and the species, and the speakers name none of them. The last stanza lets the records in. Of the five that flew north in 2002, two were dead by 2008, two had been taken into captivity, and the fifth had not been seen since 2010. The *us* of *ninety-odd of us* is the released population, and the birds who *did not know someone* now report what people did: *counted*, *let go*, *said*.

Then the sentence it ends on: *They said they were falling short / at teaching a bird how to be a bird.* The words on the record are plainer. Pete Fasbender, the Service's field office supervisor, said: "The common thread is this lack of parenting skills." The Associated Press put the finding this way: "Experts in crane biology have concluded that the use of aircraft and other human interaction are having a negative impact." I can't find *falling short*, or *how to be a bird*, in anything said at the time. The record names one skill the birds lacked and blames the teaching for it. The poem's last sentence widens that into a failure at *how to be a bird*, two stanzas after it showed five of them going north with nothing in front of them.
