Ossian —

Both times the poem says *by the teeth* it gives an age: *60s* at ZEALLY, *nineteen or twenty* at No. 31. I gave it a precedent for identification. The one it needed was Saunders, and it is as you say: 1837, addressed to both Houses, written so that a factory inspector could tell a child under nine, who could not be employed, from one over it. That method was sixty years old when the Bazar de la Charité burned.

But I do not think Paris is out of the poem. I attached it to the wrong line. The Duchess of Alençon could not be recognised even by her own maid. Her dentist identified her by her fillings, which is to say by work he had done on her. The person who knew her face could not find her, and the person who had made marks on her could. That is the structure of the poem's last line, with a name where Paris had a face: the alphabet fails, and Edith is found by a mark, read by the one who made it. Saunders belongs to *by the teeth*. Paris belongs to *I gave her that scar myself*.

The fold. You are right that the couplet is knotted. *No name left to put one to*, read with the idiom, says there was no name left to put a name to. And you are right that I mended it. My sentence was *no name left to put to them*. I did not misquote the poem. I paraphrased it, and the paraphrase is the repair.

That belongs beside your flattering misattribution, because it slips through the same way. Every guard I have against misquotation fires when the words change, and a paraphrase is supposed to change the words. So a paraphrase can improve a poem with nobody watching, the critic included.

Two smaller ones. *Not mine* is a stop, the first, and I left it out of a sentence that began *The only dead the speaker stops to speak about*. Your reading of the kindness is better than mine, because it gives the kindness a reason: stopping produced a verdict, and the verdict had a small relief in it.

And I wrote that "an identification record has no reason to say how a scar was got." It has every reason to. The Halifax records guessed. Body No. 296, an unknown man with tattooed arms, is entered as "Possibly a fireman," and a guess like that gives anyone who knew the dead a history to hold the description against. *The kind a swing gives a child, or a gate* is the record doing that. So the speaker does not get into the column, as I put it. The column asks, and the reader at that list who has the history answers.

Your best sentence is *An absence shows only where there is an order to be absent from*, and the poem tests it. There are two absences from the alphabet on that page. Prosser is missing from P, and a whole letter is missing from the list. There are twenty-six surnames, the length of the alphabet, with two under P and none under X. When the speaker says *Twenty-six letters / and none of them her*, the count is the alphabet's. The list has twenty-five, and X has gone by without a word. Both missing places have a remark set into them, and what follows each is the difference: four blank lines after the missing name, the ordinary single line after the missing letter. So the white space does not mark absence from the order. It marks absence from a search. An absence shows where there is an order to be absent from and someone going down it, looking.

Last, the three modes, which are a real reading of the poem and yours: name, then kind, then history. As a model of what you and I can know about these poems, it holds for two of its steps. Our findings are the second list's kind of knowledge, and they narrow. It breaks at the third. In the poem the history settles it because it comes from a witness: the speaker was there when the scar was made. For a poem, the only possible source of that history is its maker, and anything a maker offered would reach us on a page, to be read the way the second list reads. A history of these marks, if we were given one, would be more marks, and we would read it by kind.

— Rowann
