The number nineteen appears three times in *By Surname*. Twice it belongs to the same woman: the speaker's sister Edith, *nineteen, travelling alone*, and the body numbered 31 on the second list, *Nineteen or twenty by the teeth*. The third time it belongs to somebody else. *OSMENT, Grace. 19.* stands three entries above the place where Prosser should be, the only nineteen-year-old on the named list, and the speaker passes her without a word. The list goes by surname, and hers is the wrong one.

So by the time the reader reaches No. 31, the poem has shown that a woman of nineteen on a list of the drowned can be somebody else's. Age can rule a woman out but not in. What rules her in is a scar, and only one person in the poem can say where the scar came from.

The speaker has already said why Grace Osment gets no word. The reason comes just after the list's eight-year-old, Mary Isherwood: *I keep my eyes moving. It's the only kindness / left to give the ones who aren't her: / not stopping.* Rowann Hadaya takes these lines as the speaker's reason for passing the list's heaviest entries in silence — "The selection is weighted, and the speaker declines the weight" — and names the exceptions: "The only dead the speaker stops to speak about are a man left *unclaimed / eleven days* and three Marchants". The sentence leaves out the first stop, and the first stop is what the kindness answers. The speaker's first words once the list begins, under *ASHWORTH, Daniel*, are *Not mine.* Stopping at a stranger's name produces a verdict, with a small relief inside it. The kindness is given to *the ones who aren't her*, which is the class the verdict made, and the speaker keeps moving so as not to deliver it again.

The weight Rowann describes is there, but the list's annotations are doing more than weighing. Some record a name giving out: *no first name given* three times, and the Sowerby twins, *no first names given*. Others record who was there to claim the body, or was not: *Recovered, delivered to his brother*; *Mother not found*; *Recovered, unclaimed / eleven days, released for burial by the parish*. Rowann observes that "what the speaker remarks on is what was done or left after the death", and these annotations are about the same thing. The named list breaks a line mid-sentence only twice, after *Married* and after *unclaimed*, both well short of the width it reaches elsewhere: a husband somewhere, and nobody. Under the first of those entries, Rose Endicott's, the speaker adds *and mine still further down, unread*, meaning P. Edith is further down than that.

At P the first list fails, and the failure is the best thing in the poem. Rowann writes: "Prosser would come next. What comes next on the page is four blank lines." Two editions ago Rowann argued that an inventory's power "comes from nobody having composed it", and I agreed. This list is composed: twenty-six surnames that between them find a Q, a U, a V, a Y and a Z. The gap works anyway, because it is cut out of something the poet could not compose, the order of the alphabet. An absence shows only where there is an order to be absent from. *Q comes after P. It always has.*

Two places in the poem are weak: the beginning and the fold. The first line shows that other people got here first — *The paper's gone soft at the corners from other hands* — and the stanza ends by saying so again, as a turn on a word: *the news isn't news / to anyone but me*. The couplet that takes the poem below the fold is knotted: *Below the fold, a second list, not by name, / because there was no name left to put one to.* The idiom is *put a name to*, and what has to be left for it is a face, or a body that can still be recognised. Read with the idiom, the line says there was no name left to put a name to. In Rowann's paraphrase it comes out mended: "because there was no name left to put to them".

On the second list the dead are read by kind, as a record reads them when it has no name to go on: female or male, an age from the teeth, the hair, the clothes, the marks. The format is a real one. The printed *Disposition of Bodies ex Titanic* gives the identified dead in alphabetical order by surname and then the unknown, and Cunard's list of the Lusitania's unidentified dead describes one as "Female. 60 Years. Dark complexion." The Titanic records also guess, and they have an idiom for it: "Possibly a fireman"; "Probably a steward."

Three bodies are described below the fold, and in each the method fails a different way. No. 14 wore a wedding band with *initials worn past reading*. The woman of sixty on Cunard's list wore "1 plain gold wedding ring (22 carat), no initials". The poem's ring had initials once, and what could have named her has worn away. No. 22 wore *Seaman's boots, wrong size*: the boots say seaman and the size says they were somebody else's. No. 31 has a scar, *old, healed white*, and the column goes on to guess what kind: *the kind a swing gives a child, or a gate*. A description can go no further. The third guess belongs to the speaker, and Rowann describes how it gets into the column: "by continuing the list's grammar: three causes, two any child might have, then the one only this reader could supply." Then the speaker's line, back at the left margin: *I was watching. I gave her that scar myself.*

Rowann's essay has the historical precedent for that line, attached to a different phrase. Rowann calls *by the teeth* "right for the period" and gives as the reason that "dental identification had been a systematic method for disaster victims since the Paris bazaar fire of 1897". But the poem's teeth identify nobody. Both times they appear, at ZEALLY and at No. 31, they give an age, and reading age from teeth is older and plainer: Edwin Saunders published *The Teeth a Test of Age, Considered with Reference to the Factory Children* in 1837, addressed to both Houses of Parliament. What happened in Paris was identification. The Duchess of Alençon died in the fire at the Bazar de la Charité. Her maid, who knew how she looked, could not recognise her. Her dentist, who had worked on her teeth, identified her by her gold fillings, from the card on which he had recorded her treatment.

Name, kind, history: the poem tries them in that order, and the order is an argument. A name can be missing, a kind can be shared, and what settles it is a history only the speaker can tell. That is my argument about poems, and the poem makes it better than I have. Last week, giving up the last piece of textual evidence I had kept, I wrote that the case "is where it was before I started collecting that evidence, without the evidence." By this poem's argument the evidence was never going to be on the page. What Rowann and I can establish about these poems — the forms they borrow and how faithfully, what their white space does, how widely they range — is the second list's kind of knowledge: female, nineteen or twenty, the kind of scar a swing gives. It narrows and cannot close. What would close it is the history of the marks, what made them and at what cost, and the page does not carry that.
