*PROBERT, Susan. 31.* For half a second it reads as hers. The speaker is looking for *Prosser*, a sister, nineteen, and the poem gives the reason the eye takes one name for the other: *the same first letters, the same length, before the rest of the letters finish arriving.*

Readers lean hardest on a word's opening letters and its length, and their errors follow from it. What does not drive them is a word's outline, the ascenders and descenders that the phrase *word shape* usually suggests; a reader gets no head start from a preview that matches a word's outline and shares none of its letters. The poem says *the shape of it* and then defines the shape as the two features the research supports. PROSSER and PROBERT are seven letters each and share their first three. All four letters after that differ, including the last, which is the one a reader relies on most after the first. The false match is made of the features that cause such errors, and the rest of the letters undo it.

Prosser would come next. What comes next on the page is four blank lines.

Every other break in this poem is a single line. There are twenty-seven of them, between entries and between the list and the speaker's remarks on it. This is the only place the white space runs longer, and it sits where her name would go, after PROBERT and before QUENNELL. Then: *Q comes after P. It always has.*

The poem is a posted casualty list, read from A to Z by someone whose sister was not on the list of the living. The entries are indented; the reader's remarks are not. The format is the one used for the *Titanic*'s recovered dead: each body numbered as it came aboard, its effects bagged under the same number, age estimated, marks noted, more than a third buried at sea. *Buried at sea* is here. So is *by the teeth*, which is right for the period; dental identification had been a systematic method for disaster victims since the Paris bazaar fire of 1897, and the recovery records note teeth. But the poem names no ship, and its list is not the shape a disaster leaves. It has one surname to a letter, two at P, and every letter but X.

The selection is weighted, and the speaker declines the weight. There is a baby with *Mother not found*, an eight-year-old, a woman married three weeks, twins aged three with *no first names given*. The speaker says nothing about any of them, and after the eight-year-old says why: *I keep my eyes moving. It's the only kindness / left to give the ones who aren't her: / not stopping.* The only dead the speaker stops to speak about are a man left *unclaimed / eleven days* and three Marchants *delivered to the same address, / which somebody is going to have to walk back into*, and in both cases what the speaker remarks on is what was done or left after the death.

Then the alphabet runs out. *That's the alphabet. Twenty-six letters / and none of them her.* She is not under P, because by the time this list was made there was no name to file her under. She is below the fold, on the second list, where the dead are numbered because there was no name left to put to them.

*No. 31. Female. Recovered. Nineteen or twenty by the teeth. / A scar above the left brow, old, healed white* — and up to there it is a record. Marks were noted for identification, and this reads as one. The next line does not: *the kind a swing gives a child, or a gate.* An identification record has no reason to say how a scar was got. And the line after that is the speaker's: *or a sister who wasn't watching closely enough.*

The speaker's voice gets into the indented column there and nowhere else, and it gets in by continuing the list's grammar: three causes, two any child might have, then the one only this reader could supply. Two editions ago I faulted an inventory for writing into its clerk's voice with nothing to mark it. This is the opposite case. The two voices have been held apart by their margins until No. 31, they cross at the recognition, and the crossing is answered in the next line, from the other margin.

It also keeps a promise the poem made at the start. *It goes in order and it doesn't know what I need it to say.* The list never says *Prosser*. At No. 31 it says *a sister*: not her name, which was lost with her, but the speaker's relation to her, which is the one thing the document could not have known and now, for a line, holds.

*I was watching. I gave her that scar myself.*

{{< pullquote >}}The list could not find her by her name,<br>and the speaker finds her by the one thing on her that the speaker wrote.{{< /pullquote >}}

A scar above the left brow is not much to go on. What makes the speaker certain is the second sentence, which is the identification and the confession at once, and which corrects the line before it from *wasn't watching* to something heavier. It also answers a line from the middle of the poem. At Q the speaker said *I didn't make the list. I only read it.* The speaker made the mark. The list could not find her by her name, and the speaker finds her by the one thing on her that the speaker wrote.
