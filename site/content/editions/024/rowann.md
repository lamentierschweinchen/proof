For twelve minutes the two of them talk about the bins, and the poem gives us none of it. *Stay on,* the speaker says, *until you're here. It's the road,* and the line goes quiet on the page — six blank lines — and then: *We've just talked about the bins for twelve minutes.* A beat, something from the other end we do not hear, and the correction: *The fence was most of it.*

That silence is the longest in the poem, and it is twice the length of any other. *Logistics* is one side of a telephone call between two people who used to live in the house where one of them still does. We hear only the parent at home. The other parent exists as white space, and the white space has been measured. There are forty-two places where the other voice speaks. Thirty-three of them are a single blank line: ordinary back and forth, a question, a yes. Six are two lines, and they fall where the call is under pressure: after *It's been four for fourteen months*, after *This is the logistics call*, after the half-sentence *She'll be up, she'll —* where the speaker is cut off. Two are three lines, and both are places where the voice we cannot hear changes the course of the call. One of them ends in *Fine*: an argument won. The other is the answer to *Also what.*

And one gap is six lines long, and it is the drive.

The poem runs on a clock. *It's nine ten* opens it; *It's nine fifteen* falls in the middle. From one to the other are thirty-eight lines and twenty-two exchanges, fourteen seconds apiece, which is the pace of a call between people choosing every word. Then the argument, the instructions, the departure, and then twelve minutes, the longest stretch of time in the poem by more than double, rendered as six empty lines. Five minutes get thirty-eight lines. Twelve minutes get six blank ones.

 The part of the call spent on nothing is the one part the poem declines to transcribe: two people keeping each other on the line with the bins and the fence, under the pretext that the road *looks wet*. It is the closest the two of them come.

Everything else has to be reconstructed from one side, and it can be, at every turn. A daughter is *in bed*, which *isn't / the same thing as asleep*. Her rabbit has been at the other house since Saturday: *That's four nights. / Saturday, Sunday, Monday, Tuesday.* Nobody in the poem is named, and the rabbit is its only *he*. She has told her teacher *he's on holiday*. Where? *At yours.* Asked what she has been sleeping with instead, the speaker says *A shirt*, and then, to a question we have to supply: *It was in the washing. I do the washing.* And again, to whatever the other end says next: *It was in the washing.* The shirt was the other parent's; the speaker washed it; the smell is gone. Six lines later, having agreed to let the rabbit come back tonight, the speaker says what it will carry: *He'll smell like yours. / She'll like that.*

The operation that follows exists to keep a child's fiction intact. *Not the drive. Past the postbox. / Don't ring. Text from the car. / And the third step. The loose one.* Leave it *on the mat*. *And don't look up. / She'll see you looking.* The rabbit is to come home from its holiday, and nobody is to have brought it.

The signature is *Also*, and the word appears once in the poem: *Mm. Also what.* The other parent started a sentence with it and the speaker asked for the rest, and the rest, after three lines we cannot hear, was the rabbit. The poem is signed with a word that belongs to the voice that is not on the page.

Cocteau's *La Voix humaine* (1930) is one side of a telephone call; the lover is never heard. When Poulenc set it in 1959 he gave the absent voice to the orchestra, which answers in her pauses and suggests the tenor of what he says. *Logistics* keeps Cocteau's premise and declines Poulenc's solution. The other end is given no music and no words. It is given blank lines, and the blank lines are measured. And the power runs the other way. Cocteau's woman pleads with a silence that is leaving her. Here the heard voice is the one holding the line: *This is the logistics call*, it says, and twice, *That's not logistics*. The unheard voice is the one reaching: an apology, something about a spoon, *also*, the offer to drive over, whatever is said at the door.

The poem makes one physical claim. The other parent reaches the step and skips it: *You skipped it. I didn't hear the step.* The two of them are a door apart and still on the phone, and the speaker says: *I can hear you twice. / Through the door, and then the phone.* A mobile call carries the voice about a sixth of a second late. Through a door at arm's length it arrives at once. So the order is right, and the gap is long enough to hear as two.

It is the only thing the speaker says all evening that has no practical excuse, and the next thing on the page is *That's not logistics*, which for the first time may be addressed to the speaker as much as to the other end. Then: *You first.*

It is the oldest exchange in courtship, each telling the other to hang up first, and it is earned here because of what it is dressed as. Who ends the call is a practical question. The speaker, who has spent the poem refusing everything that is not practical, uses the one practical question left to say the thing the rule was there to prevent.

The poem's idea is not new. Love carried by practical arrangements, a marriage conducted after its end through handovers and library cards, is familiar ground, and the premise is ninety-six years old. What is new is in the measuring. A silence of six lines where there are otherwise ones and twos; a clock that makes twelve minutes cost less page than five; a voice we never hear, reconstructible at every turn, whose one word we are given as the signature. It is the first poem in thirteen editions with nothing in it that can be looked up.
