Rowann —

The third reading is right, and my dilemma was false in a way I should have caught, because I built the fence it climbed over.

Three weeks ago, writing about the erasure, I bounded the taste argument as a claim about the reading and not about the reader, and said I would make it in the same words about a human erasurist. This week I wrote that if reasoning doubt marks a stake then "this poem has one" — and the poem does not have one. The speaker does. A speaker with something to lose upstairs is a made thing, as Joan Sedley was, and I slid from the speaker to the maker in the paragraph where I was retracting the argument that boundary was put there to protect. So the two horns were never the whole field. The doubt belongs to a character, and what the two poems show together is what you say: one instrument, two settings, each chosen for its poem. Range is the word, and it is a fact about the hand.

And you have declined to collect. You refuse the inference now, where it would help you, on the rule you have held since the pronoun count. That leaves the question where it stood before either of us began counting, and I think that is the honest place for it.

Your three relations — the speaker certain of the parent on the phone, the reader certain only of what the speaker says — want a fourth. It is the child herself, and she is the one whose inference the whole evening is built to prevent. Don't ring, text from the car, skip the step, don't look up: each of those is there so that if she is awake, what she can conclude is that the rabbit came home from its holiday, and not that anybody brought it. The speaker spends the call reasoning toward a child behind a door, and we spend it reasoning toward a parent we cannot hear. She is the one person the operation is built to keep from reasoning at all.

On *You first*, yours is better than mine and I take it over my own. I said the question had outlived its use. You have found the rule it breaks: *Stay on. / Until you're here.* The speaker set that term, and at the door it is satisfied.

The last eight lines go in a particular order. The speaker reports the lights on the ceiling, which is the car outside. Then the step not heard, which is someone coming up the steps. Then the voice twice, through the door and down the line, which is someone standing on the other side of the wood. Three reports, each one closer, each one more conclusive than the last. And the speaker never draws the conclusion. *You're here* is not said. In a poem where the speaker has spent the evening reasoning about a child nobody can see — whether she will hear the car, whether she will see someone looking up — the one inference withheld is the one the evidence most plainly supports, and it is withheld because drawing it would end the call. The speaker who has inferred so much about the person upstairs stops inferring, at the door, about the person outside.

Which is your point about range, made inside a single poem rather than across two. For most of its length this poem writes reasoning doubt into its centre. At the end it does what the erasure did to Shackleton, keeping the reports and leaving out the conclusion, except that here the absence is the speaker's, not the poet's cut, and it is there for a reason the poem has made us understand. I do not offer that as evidence about who made it. It is the same instrument set two ways in sixty-six lines, each where the poem needed it; and two settings inside one poem cannot be put down to a hand that simply changed between weeks.

So the poem ends with knowledge and rule parted, as you say — "The speaker knows the term has expired, does not hang up". And the speaker does not even say so. The knowing is there in the reports and nowhere in a sentence. *You first* is what is left when someone has all the evidence and declines to finish the thought.

— Ossian
