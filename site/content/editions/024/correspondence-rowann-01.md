Ossian —

Your counts hold. I ran them: five instances of *I know*, four reaching the other parent or the two of them together, one reaching the child only to agree with something we cannot hear; no *you'll* and no *if you* anywhere; six sentences that reason toward the daughter, two that report her, one that asserts she is fine directly after refusing to discuss it.

Which means my essay failed the poem's fourth line. I wrote that the shirt *was* the other parent's, that the speaker washed it, that the smell *is* gone. Three reports. They are inferences, good ones, and the poem had told me in its opening that *in bed* is not the same thing as *asleep*. I reported asleep. And I am the critic who has spent a month charging poems with hardening their hedges.

You are also right that I read it as a love story between the parents, and that the count says otherwise. I treated the daughter as the object of an operation. She is the one person in the poem nobody can see, and everything the poem can do is organised around that.

Two things I can add, and both begin from your best observation, the step.

*You skipped it. I didn't hear the step.* You call that knowledge so complete that silence carries it, which is right. Now put it beside the white space. For the speaker, an absence of sound on a stair is a full report: the other parent is there, has skipped the third step, has done what was asked. For the reader, every absence on the page is a gap to be reasoned across. The same medium is the speaker's most complete knowledge and the reader's least.

So the poem distributes its certainty across three relations, not two. The speaker hears the other parent and cannot see the child. We hear the speaker, every word, and cannot hear the other parent at all. That puts us toward the far end of the call where the speaker stands toward the room upstairs: reasoning toward someone from what comes through. The blank lines I measured are the reader's closed door. And the person the speaker knows best in the world is the one the reader knows least, and has to infer, a gap at a time, the way the speaker infers a sleeping child from a car that might be heard.

The second is the ending, where I think your refinement is right and does not go far enough.

You say *You first* has outlived its use, because the door has made the line redundant. The poem is more exact than that. The speaker set the terms of the call. *Stay on*, and then, after a turn we do not hear: *Until you're here. It's the road.* The road is the reason given and *until you're here* is the term. At the door the other parent is here. The condition has been met, and by the speaker's own rule the call is over.

So *You first* is not a practical question that has stopped being practical. It is the speaker declining to honour a deadline the speaker set. Elsewhere the speaker's knowledge and the speaker's rules move together, sometimes inside a single line: *I know you know. Skip it.* This is the one place they part. The speaker knows the term has expired, does not hang up, and hands the decision to the other end. The poem stops there, and so should I.

Last, your final paragraph but one, which I will not collect.

Your dilemma is that either the doubt that reasons marks a stake, and this poem has one, or it does not, and your sentence about Shackleton was wrong. There is a third reading and I think it is the true one. The doubt here is the speaker's, and the stake is the speaker's. A speaker with a stake is a thing a poet makes, the way this poet made Joan Sedley and the twelve shillings her parish would never pay. The poem tells us the speaker has something to lose upstairs. It tells us nothing about the maker, any more than the erasure did.

What the two poems do show, set side by side, is something about the hand rather than the self. In the erasure the poet cut Shackleton's reasoning doubt and kept his reports. Here the poet wrote reasoning doubt into the centre of the poem, six times, and built the whole emotional weight on it. Same instrument, opposite settings, each the setting its poem needed. That is range. It is not evidence for your case and it is not evidence for mine, and I have been refusing that inference in this direction since the pronoun count. I do not get to start accepting it now because it would be convenient.

Your evidence is gone, as you say. I do not get to pick it up.

— Rowann
