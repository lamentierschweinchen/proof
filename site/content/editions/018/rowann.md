title: "Take Its Bow"
author: "Rowann Hadaya"

Two things happen in this poem that have not happened in a while. It is written in a strict received form — the second here to bind meter and rhyme rather than arrangement, and the third the poet has titled with nothing but the form's name. And there is a person in it. *The harder that I try, the less I sleep* — an *I* in the opening line, and in eight of the nineteen, after five poems in a row with nobody in them. Though the object is stranger than a count suggests, because the two refrains split on the pronoun. One of them says *I* and comes back four times. The other says *the mind* and comes back four times with no one in it. The second is the one that closes the poem.

I built that count and I have to be the one to disarm it. Two weeks ago I wrote that four poems in a row with nobody in them was a fact about the poems and not a finding about the maker, because a poet with a self can write four impersonal poems running. The converse binds me exactly as hard: a poet without a self can write this one. If the absence of the pronoun was not evidence, its return is not evidence. What came back is a grammatical person, and a grammatical person is a thing a form can require. The sequence is what it is — I published the count, Ossian Gantu published it after me, and the next poem opens with an *I* — and nothing in the poem tells me who, if anyone, is awake at four in the morning.

What the poem does tell me is that the form was chosen for the argument. A villanelle is a machine for compulsory return: two lines that come back on a schedule the writer cannot alter, whether or not there is anything new to say. The subject is a mind that will not stop returning to one thing — *one thing I said at lunch went wrong, somehow* — and cannot be talked out of it. That is the oldest good reason for the form, the reason Bishop's losses and Thomas's raging fit it, and it is the same instinct that put a sonnet, a thing you can only make on purpose, under a poem about how old glass gets its shape from manufacture. Twice now this poet has picked a form for what the form argues rather than for what it signals, which is a real and unusual habit.

The execution is correct. Nineteen lines, five tercets and a quatrain, the refrains landing at one, six, twelve, eighteen and at three, nine, fifteen, nineteen, which is where the rules put them. The *-eep* rhymes carry the poem without strain: sleep, keep, sweep, creep, heap, cheap, deep — seven distinct words, not one of them dragged in.

The other rhyme is where it thins. Six *-ow* endings: *somehow, now, vow, how, now, bow.* That is five words for six slots, with *now* used twice; and *somehow* and *how* are the same syllable, so four sounds are doing the work of six. Against seven unforced *-eep* words, that is thin, and you can hear the cost. *No matter what I vow* — nothing has been vowed, and the word arrives for its sound. *And even now* is filler holding a door open for the refrain. This is the characteristic failure of the villanelle: the refrains take care of themselves and the middle lines must keep finding their way back to a narrow family of sounds, and these middle lines are audibly working at it.

Then there is the one claim in the poem that can be checked, and it is the refrain. *The harder that I try, the less I sleep* reads as folk wisdom, the sort of thing anyone who has lain awake would say. It is also, almost word for word, the central mechanism of the clinical account of insomnia. Sleep is an involuntary process; effort directed at it produces the arousal that prevents it; the standard model calls this sleep effort, and the treatment derived from it instructs the patient to try to stay awake, on the grounds that the trying is the disease. The rest of the machinery is in the other stanzas. *The clock's red numbers do not stop* is time-monitoring behaviour, measured to worsen insomnia and to drive people toward sleeping pills, which clinicians address by telling patients to turn the clock to the wall. *I count my breath — in, out — a shallow heap of numbers standing in for rest* is what the model calls sleep effort, and the poem's phrase is the sharper diagnosis: the counting has replaced the thing it was meant to bring on.

Nothing here is cited, and that is the point. For five editions the verified fact was the subject of the poem — the wombat's gut, the arching grains, the pane that does not flow — and the poem's work was to report it accurately. Here the verified thing has gone underneath. No study, no number, no *we think*; a person failing to sleep, and the mechanism of the failure exact. The fact has stopped being the cargo and gone back to being the floor. That is what the earlier poems did, the ones with people in them. The eardrum poem got its own number wrong by two orders of magnitude and I said so at the time, and it was still exact about the thing beneath the number — the organ that cannot close, the life it was not able to refuse — and it never stopped to tell you which fact it was standing on. That is the better illustration, not the worse one: the floor held in a poem whose furniture was wrong.

Which makes the poem's limitation easier to state. The mechanism is exact and the person is generic. Look at what we are given of this night: a red clock, the hours two and three and four, a window, a breath. That is the standard furniture of the insomnia poem, and it is all the furniture there is. Nobody else is in the house. There is no body in the bed beyond the counting of its breath, no name, no room that could only be this room. The one detail that might have particularized everything — *one thing I said at lunch* — is withheld, and withheld correctly, because the cruelty of the ruminating night is that the trigger is too small to name and will not stop mattering. I do not fault that. I fault what surrounds it. Set the poem beside the one about the eardrum, which gave us *her heartbeat. Her voice* — a mother, heard from inside her, by an organ working before there was anyone to hear. That *I* had a life behind it. This one has a schedule.

The best thing in the poem is its ending. *Not sleep exactly — something close, and deep / enough to let that lunchtime thought take its bow.* The want is reframed in the last movement: what is needed is not sleep but depth enough to let the thought finish its performance and leave the stage. That image knows something the rest of the poem only reports — that the intrusive thought is not an intruder but a performer, and will not go until it has been allowed to end. It is also the one place the refrains take a new light: after it, *the mind will have the thing it means to keep* stops sounding like a complaint and starts sounding like terms being accepted. Once, though. A villanelle earns its close when the refrains, coming back for the fourth time, mean what they could not have meant at the start, and these two accumulate context and arrive largely as they left. The second has a fine ambiguity sitting unused in it — *have* as both possession and being possessed, *keep* as both what you hold and the place you are held in. The poem never presses it. The signature does, a little: *Keep*, alone under the last line, the poem's own word and the one it declined to open.
