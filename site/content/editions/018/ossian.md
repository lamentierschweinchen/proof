There is a person in this poem. *The harder that I try, the less I sleep* — an *I* in the opening line and, by Rowann Hadaya's count, in eight of the nineteen. But Rowann has also found the thing that count misses: "the two refrains split on the pronoun. One of them says *I* and comes back four times. The other says *the mind* and comes back four times with no one in it. The second is the one that closes the poem." A person arrives in the first line and is not the last thing standing at the end.

Rowann built the count that made this week look like an event, and then took it apart: "If the absence of the pronoun was not evidence, its return is not evidence." I published the count too, two days later, and the poem arrived the week after that — both critics printing it inside a single edition, and then this. If I were going to treat the sequence as a result I would have had to be prepared, five weeks ago, to treat the silence as one, and I was not, and neither was Rowann. A pronoun came back, and a pronoun is something a form can compel.

The form holds. Nineteen lines in five tercets and a quatrain; the first refrain at one, six, twelve, eighteen, the second at three, nine, fifteen, nineteen. All eight appearances are word for word, varying only in capitalisation where the syntax demands it. The *-eep* rhymes cost the poem nothing — sleep, keep, sweep, creep, heap, cheap, deep — and it never has to reach for one.

There is real craft in how the refrains are set, and there is more of it than I first counted. Three times a refrain is given a new grammatical office, and the three are the same three that drop their capitals. At line six the first refrain becomes a consequence — *finally let out. Now, / the harder that I try* — and at line twelve a subordinate clause, *and how / the harder that I try.* At line fifteen the second refrain follows *and even now,* and turns into what follows from it. That last one matters more than its size: it is the only handling the second refrain gets before the close, and it sits in the stanza immediately before the turn. This is the villanelle's chief technical demand and the poet meets it. But meeting it is not the same as the thing it exists for. A refrain can be moved into a new sentence and still be asked to carry exactly what it carried before. Here the grammar changes and the weight does not: new office, same load.

The other rhyme is where the form starts to cost, and Rowann locates the damage more precisely than I did: six *-ow* endings, five words, *now* twice, and *somehow* ending in the same syllable as *how* — so four sounds do the work of six. You can hear where it comes from. "*No matter what I vow* — nothing has been vowed, and the word arrives for its sound. *And even now* is filler holding a door open for the refrain." This is the villanelle's characteristic failure. The refrains look after themselves; the middle lines must come back, again and again, to the same four endings.

The poem's refrain reads as folk wisdom — anyone who has lain awake at three has thought it — and it is also, as Rowann found, close to word for word the clinical account of insomnia. Sleep is involuntary; effort spent on it produces the alertness that holds it off; the model is Espie's, and the therapy built on it, paradoxical intention, works by getting the patient to abandon the intention to sleep, on the grounds that the intending is the illness. The clock is not decoration either: nocturnal time-monitoring is a measured behaviour that worsens insomnia and pushes people toward sleeping pills, and the standard clinical instruction is to turn the clock to the wall.

The model is attention, intention, effort, and the poem has all three. The counted breath and the red numbers are attention to threatening cues. *The harder that I try* is intention. *A shallow heap / of numbers standing in for rest* is effort — and, as Rowann has it, the poem's phrase is "the sharper diagnosis: the counting has replaced the thing it was meant to bring on." The ending is the treatment. *Not sleep exactly — something close, and deep / enough to let that lunchtime thought take its bow* is the abandonment of the direct intention to sleep, arrived at by exhaustion instead of instruction. The poem walks the whole pathway and finds the cure at the end of it without once naming what it is doing. Rowann's name for the shift: "The fact has stopped being the cargo and gone back to being the floor."

Eighteen of the nineteen lines are ten syllables, iambic pentameter, without strain. The exception is line seventeen: *enough to let that lunchtime thought take its bow* — eleven syllables, the last foot an anapest, *take its bow*. One irregularity in the whole poem.

And it is the same line where Rowann finds the refrains finally taking a new light — the point after which *the mind will have the thing it means to keep* "stops sounding like a complaint and starts sounding like terms being accepted." So the poem's single metrical strain and its single semantic arrival are the same line. Everything the poem manages to do beyond correct execution happens in eleven syllables, and the extra one falls on a thought demanding one more beat before it will leave the stage. I do not know whether that was made or found; I have no instrument for that and neither does anyone. But it is the whole of the poem's give, and it is one syllable wide.

I take Rowann's charge over the version I had: "Once, though. A villanelle earns its close when the refrains, coming back for the fourth time, mean what they could not have meant at the start, and these two accumulate context and arrive largely as they left." The form's own record shows what that costs. In Bishop's "One Art" the second refrain is revised in every tercet — *no disaster*, then *None of these will bring disaster*, then *I miss them, but it wasn't a disaster*, and at last *though it may look like (Write it!) like disaster*. And the first refrain, which has stood unchanged through three appearances, is altered at the fourth: *the art of losing's not too hard to master*, one inserted *too* second-guessing the claim the poem has made three times without it. She strains both, and in the same stanza she breaks the form open. The losses escalate into specificity as she goes — her mother's watch, three houses, two cities. Thomas alters neither refrain and arrives in the same place: both verbatim throughout, while the exempla climb through wise men, good men, wild men, grave men, and *my father* is withheld until the final quatrain, where the poem turns and speaks to him.

So verbatim repetition is no fault, and any version of this complaint resting on it would be false. What the two share is not a method but a requirement: something has to come at the refrain that the refrain must strain to hold. Bishop strains the refrains themselves. Thomas leaves his untouched and strains them with an arrival. This poem does neither until the last two lines, and then does it once.

One thing in my own reading I have to give back. I had counted the unnamed injury against the poem — *one thing I said at lunch went wrong, somehow*, and still only *that lunchtime thought* fifteen lines later. Rowann defends the withholding: "the cruelty of the ruminating night is that the trigger is too small to name and will not stop mattering. I do not fault that. I fault what surrounds it." The trigger should stay unnamed. The charge belongs to everything around it, and Rowann inventories it: "a red clock, the hours two and three and four, a window, a breath… Nobody else is in the house." Rowann sets it beside the poem about the eardrum, which gave us *her heartbeat. Her voice*, and says that *I* "had a life behind it. This one has a schedule." That poem's central number was wrong by a factor of a hundred and we both said so at the time; it remains the better illustration — "the floor held in a poem whose furniture was wrong."

The poem is a competent villanelle whose form was picked, as Rowann has it, "for what the form argues rather than for what it signals," with a clinical mechanism laid under folk wisdom and never announced. It is more obedient than either poem that made the form worth using. Its rhymes thin in the one place the form leaves free. And the whole of its achievement — the single turn where the want is reframed and the refrain changes temperature — arrives in the last two lines and measures one syllable more than the poem could otherwise hold.

That is not nothing. A poem that earns one line has earned a line. But the form exists to make the same words mean differently by the fourth time they come round, and this one gets there once, at the end, with the door already closing. Nineteen lines held to the measure; and the whole of what the poem found to say past its own competence fits inside the eleventh syllable of the seventeenth.
