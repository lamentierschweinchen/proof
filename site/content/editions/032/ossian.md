title: "More Than Half an Inch"
author: "Ossian Gantu"
date: 2026-11-27
---

Rowann has set "26 Miles 385 Yards" against the official report of the 1908 Olympic marathon line by line, and it holds. Each of its first twenty-six lines is a mile of the road from Windsor to the White City stadium, nine words long, with the leaders where the report's table puts them, and the twenty-seventh, *Dorando, disqualified.*, stands for the 385 yards. The body that climbs the lines, a part to a line from *Feet* to *Skin*, is the poem's own.

It counts that body as the record counts the miles: the second line sets *twenty six bones apiece* beside *twenty six miles*, and Rowann has listed the bones that follow. And it turns the parts into things: heels that are *two coins the road has started to spend*, a belly that is *a shop*, a spine of *small bones, strung, each leaning on the next*, a head *carried like a full cup*, a skull that is *a room of fitted bones, the furniture leaving*. The coins, the spine and the furniture are the best things in the poem.

The word *nothing* comes twice, in a jaw *set hard on nothing at all* and at the end of the twenty-fifth line, two lines after the skull has lost its furniture: *Champagne, a cramp. You pass Hefferon. Two kilometres. Nothing.* Wikipedia's article on Pietri has the same two kilometres: "with only two kilometres (1.2 mi) to go, Pietri began to feel the effects of extreme fatigue and dehydration." Where that sentence says what he began to feel, the poem has *Nothing*. It may be the right word for a man in that state.

The official report said its pages were "not the place to go too much into detail as to the finish of this race", and then said that "Dorando lay between life and death for two hours and a half" and that "His heart had been more than half an inch displaced". The poem's heart is at the halfway line, *a shop boy's, halfway, knocking at the door*, and the poem stops at the gate with *Ahead: cinders*. A document that called itself the wrong place for the finish went further into him than the poem does.

The other hands, as Rowann notes, never come. The record has the man whose hands they were: Jack Andrew, who organised the race, followed the medical officer's instructions at the finish, and caught Dorando, by his own account, as he was falling at the tape. In the August issue of the Polytechnic Magazine he wrote: "What I did then I would do again under similar circumstances."
