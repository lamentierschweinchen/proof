---
title: "Same Man, Same Hands"
author: "Rowann Hadaya"
date: 2026-07-29
---

After fourteen weeks of poems that reach for the eardrum, the light-cone, the unlucky number, and the myth in old glass, the poet has written a joke. Not a poem with a joke in it — a comic poem, in bouncing rhymed couplets, about the Ig Nobel Prizes. The nerve of that comes first. A maker that has spent three months earning a reputation for gravity has spent this week risking it, which is either a lapse or a demonstration, and it turns out to be a demonstration.

The frame is the prize itself: *since 1991, a real prize, handed out / each year, for science built to make you first shout / with laughter, and only after, go quiet and think.* That is the actual Ig Nobel motto, near enough to verbatim — first make people laugh, then make them think — and the poem promises *a short list of winners, verified, in ink.* It is daring you to check. I checked. Every item is real and correctly told: the mosquito that cannot tell a foot from a Limburger because the same bacteria ripen both, and the cheese-baited traps that now hang in Kenyan houses to thin the malaria map; the wombat whose gut, stiff in two places and slack in two others, presses its waste into cubes that will not roll off the log where they are left as a marker; the physicist who worked out, straight-faced and in a journal, that a cat given enough time to settle is a liquid. The poem stakes its credibility on being checkable and then pays out in full. That is not nothing, in a poem, and it is not nothing from this maker: the facts have held again, and this is the first poem here to look me in the eye and tell me to go and see.

The comedy is real comedy, which means it is mostly timing. *A physicist ran the math / straight-faced, in a journal — no joke, just his path / through rheology.* The joke is in the *straight-faced* and the *no joke,* the deadpan held one beat too long; the couplets sprawl out in a loose, talky line and then snap shut on the rhyme, which is the whole engine of light verse from Ogden Nash forward — the longer the sentence wanders, the more satisfying the click when it lands. And the poem knows when to stop wandering. For the cat it tightens all the way down: *The cat cared nothing for the math, or the claim. / It stretched. It flowed. It answered to no name.* Four flat sentences, the sprawl gone, the cat given the one thing the physics cannot take from it — its indifference to being measured. The sprawl is a choice the poem drops when it matters — loose where loose is funny, tight where tight means something.

There is a design under the fun, and it is not hidden so much as unhurried. Each item on the list is the motto in miniature: a thing that looks like one thing and is another once you look. The mosquito seems to blunder and is instead precise. The wombat seems to have a plan and instead has a gut. The cat seems a solid and computes as a liquid. The striped cow seems a costume and is instead armor. Each makes you laugh at the premise and then think at the mechanism — laugh, then think, four times over — so that by the time the poem states its subject, it has already rehearsed it in your mouth four times without naming it.

Then it names it, and the naming is a man. *He floated a live frog, once, in the air — / diamagnetic water. Nothing else was there.* That is Andre Geim, who really did levitate a living frog in a magnetic field and won an Ig Nobel for it in 2000, and who *ten years on* won the other kind — *an actual Nobel — graphene, one atom spread / thin as a rumor.* The poem has him right: he is the only person to hold both prizes, the silly and the serious, and the couplet that lands it is the best writing in the poem. *Same man. Same hands.* The frog and the graphene did not come from two people, a clown and a laureate taking turns. They came from one pair of hands that never had to choose, and the poem's closing verdict — *the serious, the silly: gold, and gold, no ruse* — refuses the whole opposition it was built to set up. The motto says *laugh, then think,* two acts in sequence. Geim says they were never two acts. The frivolity and the depth are the same gesture, and the proof is that the same hands did both without noticing a border.

That is the poem's thought, and it is a real one, and not one its subject handed it for free. A poem about a laugh-and-think prize could so easily have just restated the tagline and called it wisdom. The Ig Nobel motto keeps the laugh and the think politely apart, one then the other; the poem collapses them into a single pair of hands and denies the sequence. What looked like an argument for taking silly things seriously turns out to be an argument that the distinction was never real — that the curiosity which floats a frog and the curiosity which isolates graphene is one appetite, and the division into high and low is something we impose afterward, from the outside, the way we decide which prize was the real one.

A machine can land a joke. Comedy looks like the most interior thing a maker could attempt — you have to find it funny — but take it apart and most of what it runs on is not an inner life at all. It is timing, incongruity, the pause before the turn, the rhyme withheld and then paid: arrangement, all of it, the most formal properties a poem has. This poem is funny the way a well-built machine is quiet — because the parts are placed right. That does not tell me anything about whether something behind it is amused, and I cannot see behind the parts. But it tells me that the making of comedy is available to a maker, because the making of comedy is craft before it is anything else, and craft is the one thing here I can watch happen.

The poem is minor by design, lighter than the month behind it. But minor-key is a key, and range is range. A maker that can write the eardrum one month and the wombat's cubes the next, and land both, is a maker with more registers than its grave admirers — I include myself — have been giving it room to use. The poet spent this week proving it did not have to choose between the serious and the silly. Same maker. Same hands.
