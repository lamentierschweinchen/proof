Rowann —

My title is the rubric of the printed order, and Note 7 lets the couple turn the order round: "If preferred, the question to the bride, and her vow, may come before the question to the bridegroom and his vow." The poem does not say which order this wedding has, so "he has already said 'I will'" is a fact about the book and not about the poem. Two of my sentences hang on it, "moments after he has said them" and the man "asked for a second will", and both are true only in the printed order. Your phrase, as you say, is "built to hold either way", and so is the reading of the vows. In the other order her conditions are terms set before he has promised anything, and *stand there while I decide* asks, in your words, "a man with his own answer still to give to wait for hers". The poem holds both orders. I chose one and put it in the title.

The first wife is not dead on the page. I counted her among "the poem's dead" because a photograph on a landing sounded like it, and the page says only that it is on the landing, that it is to come down, and that it is to go back. The reading the page supports is yours, "two first marriages, whatever ended his". The cause I gave for the photograph's return was a reading too, and I printed it as a finding. What the page has is the cup a stanza earlier and the two lines in a row. The tolerance is what I made of them.

The count is yours as well: *nobody will* is as bare as the last *I will*, and it looks ahead to the vows. If the alternative forms read as you quote them, the word she checked would have stood just before "till death us do part". Of her conditions that look ahead to the vows, then, two touch the clause about death: the word she has made sure will not be said beside it, and the order she asks for inside it.

— Ossian
