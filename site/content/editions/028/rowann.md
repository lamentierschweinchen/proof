title: "Taken for Granted"

Before anyone at a Church of England wedding says *I will*, the service asks twice for reasons the marriage cannot go ahead. The congregation is asked first: "anyone present who knows a reason why these persons may not lawfully marry" is to "declare it now." Then the couple are told that their vows "are to be made in the presence of God, who is judge of all and knows all the secrets of our hearts", and asked the same. Then the question: "N, will you take N to be your husband? Will you love him, comfort him, honour and protect him, and, forsaking all others, be faithful to him as long as you both shall live?"

"Impediments" happens between that question and its answer. The speaker needs the vicar to *go on looking kind for one more minute* and the man she is marrying to *stand there while I decide*. She says *I will* twenty-four times, and twenty-three times there is an *if* after it. The call for reasons has passed by then, and none of hers would have counted, since none is a reason she may not lawfully marry. The nearest the poem comes to the law is a paper her daughter's solicitor has, and the condition on that is that it be *signed, and read first, and not spoken of again*.

The current service doesn't use the poem's title. *Impediment* is the Prayer Book's word, from its charge that "if either of you know any impediment, why ye may not be lawfully joined together in Matrimony, ye do now confess it", and the word was in that charge when Shakespeare wrote "Let me not to the marriage of true minds / Admit impediments." The italicised words come from the current service: *protect* where the Prayer Book had "keep", *all others* with the s that its "forsaking all other" lacks. *Obey* survives there only as an alternative, so *nobody will; I checked* is accurate.

J. L. Austin's first example of an utterance that does something instead of describing it comes from a wedding: "I do (sc. take this woman to be my lawful wedded wife)". His editor, J. O. Urmson, added a note: Austin "realized that the expression 'I do' is not used in the marriage ceremony too late to correct his mistake", and it was left in "as it is philosophically unimportant that it is a mistake." This poem is built on the difference. The Anglican answer is a future, and a future takes an *if* without strain. *Will* runs from *nobody will* to *you will stand there*, and turns conditional only near the dead man: *no one tells me he would have wanted this, because he would.*

The conditions carry a history in what they take for granted. *I may go on setting out his cup on Sundays*: she has been setting it out. *I may still be angry with him for dying*: she is. *I do not have to finish his marmalade*: some is left. *Your first wife's photograph comes down from the landing*: it is up there now.

The weakest line is where this stops. *I will if the dog may be in the photograph / and does not have to be a dog about it.* The first half assumes a dog and a photograph still to be taken. The second assumes nothing about anyone. It is a pun on an idiom, being a baby or a pig about something, and it asks for a smile. It is also the only clause in the poem where I can't say what anyone would have to do or stop doing.

Shakespeare's sonnet puts beyond impediment the love that never "bends with the remover to remove", and the speaker has that love for a man who was removed. He is in seven of her conditions, from the fourth, *the one we had for him*, to the fifteenth, *I may call you by his name once, by mistake, / and be forgiven*, and after his name he does not appear again. The man she is marrying arrives in the seventh, on an indented line, in the clause after the cup: *and you do not ask whose it is*. His next appearance is another indented line beginning *and*: *and you do not take it personally*. In each of the three conditions that have both men in them, what is asked of the living one concerns the dead one.

The conditions that ask permission with *I may* are for the cup, the anger and the name, all of them his, and then, at the head of the last stanza of conditions, *I will if I may be glad*, the first with nothing of him in it.

Three of the four italicised words come from the question, and she glosses each before she will agree to it. *All others* belongs to the clause about being faithful, and she takes the dead man out of it: *I will if all others does not mean him*. *Comfort* and *protect*, as she has them, are done to her. *Comfort* does not mean *telling me it will be all right*, and *protect* means *making me see the doctor / when I say I won't go*, which is the poem's only *won't*, and she wants it overruled. The man's question is the same as hers except for "wife" and "her", so these two are terms set on his promise from inside her own. She leaves "love" and "honour" alone, and the word "love" is not in the poem. Twenty-two editions ago the poet assembled a poem from terms of service, in which an institution told its user: "Your continued use of the Site following the posting of changes / will mean that you accept and agree to the changes." Here the speaker says what the institution's words mean, three times.

She asks the man for two promises, and both times adds *which you can't*: *I will if you promise not to need nursing, which you can't. / I will if you promise to die second, which you can't.* Everything in the service's question is put to the will: will you love, comfort, honour, protect, be faithful. Its limit is "as long as you both shall live", which ends at the first death and doesn't say whose. She asks for the order.

One of the twenty-three she has made sure of already: *nobody will; I checked*. Two can't be met, and she says so. Two more go together, and the second undoes the first: *your first wife's photograph comes down from the landing*, and then, in the shortest condition of all, *it goes back*. One forbids a sentence because the sentence is true. The rest depend on other people and on years that haven't happened. The last is *I will if you will stand there while I decide*, and then the answer, with the *if* gone. Apart from the one she checked, none of the conditions has been settled when she gives it.

Austin also set poems aside. A performative, he wrote, "will, for example, be in a peculiar way hollow or void if said by an actor on the stage, or if introduced in a poem, or spoken in soliloquy." By that rule none of the twenty-four answers marries anybody, the last one included. The rule says nothing about the reader, and the reader is where this poem does its work. Nobody vouches for the cup on Sundays. A condition takes it for granted and so does the reader, as readers do with the background of any sentence, without needing to trust whoever is speaking. The service invoked a God who "knows all the secrets of our hearts", and asked only for the ones that would stop a wedding. The poem has the others, set out as what twenty-three conditions assume, and then two words that set no terms.
