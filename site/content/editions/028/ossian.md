In the Church of England's marriage service the man is asked first. The rubric reads "The minister says to the bridegroom", then the question, then "He answers", and only after that "The minister says to the bride". Rowann Hadaya puts the poem where it happens: "'Impediments' happens between that question and its answer." The question is hers, so by the time the poem begins he has already said "I will", and the poem does not print it.

Two of her terms bear on what he has just said. *I will if comfort does not mean telling me it will be all right. / I will if protect means making me see the doctor / when I say I won't go.* Comfort and protect are what the minister asked him to do for her, and Rowann sees that these are "terms set on his promise from inside her own". His promise was made without terms, and she tells him what his words mean moments after he has said them. She is as exact about the one legal paper in the poem, which must be *signed, and read first*; if it is the agreement it sounds like, "a full appreciation of its implications" is what the Supreme Court said in 2010 such an agreement needs before a court will give it effect.

Two of her terms are impossible, and the poem marks both with the same three words. *I will if you promise not to need nursing, which you can't. / I will if you promise to die second, which you can't.* Rowann's reading of the second is that "She asks for the order", since the declaration's limit "ends at the first death and doesn't say whose". Both terms reach further, into the part of the service that has not happened yet. After the declarations come the vows, and the vows say "in sickness and in health, to love and to cherish, till death us do part". Her two impossible terms answer the first and the last of them. She asks him to promise away the sickness and to settle the death that the vows exist to promise through.

The last condition asks him for something too. *I will if you will stand there while I decide.* The man who has already said "I will" is asked for a second will, which is to wait. The poem has used the word in other senses on the way: a forecast she has checked, *nobody will*; a comfort she refuses in advance, *it will be all right*; and his, *you will stand there*. Then she gives hers, and it is the only *will* in the poem with nothing after it.

Both of the poem's dead are in her conditions, and she treats them alike. Her husband's cup stays: *I will if I may go on setting out his cup on Sundays / and you do not ask whose it is.* The groom's first wife is in a photograph on the landing, and that condition goes one way and then comes back: *I will if your first wife's photograph comes down from the landing. / I will if it goes back.* Rowann writes that "the second undoes the first". What undoes it is the tolerance she has asked for her own dead.

Rowann has also found the weakest line, and given the reason for it: the dog that *does not have to be a dog about it* is "the only clause in the poem where I can't say what anyone would have to do or stop doing."

The poem ends on the declaration. In the service the vows come after it, and in them she will promise, herself, to stay through the two things she has just asked him to promise away.
