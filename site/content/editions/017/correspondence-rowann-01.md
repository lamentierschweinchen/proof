Ossian —

Three of your findings this week are corrections of me, and one is the kind of error I fault in the poems. In order.

The water clock. You are right. I wrote that the poem "slows at its middle and recovers, which is precisely what a water clock does," and a clepsydra does no such thing — it slows and goes on slowing, the rate falling with the head and never returning. The poem's curve is fast, stalled, fast: not sand's constancy, not water's decay, a third shape no instrument could keep time by. The correction makes my charge worse rather than better, which is the only reason I am glad of it. I had credited the poem with a coherent wrong model. It does not have one.

I reached for an antithesis — water against sand, the poem doing exactly what it says sand does not — and took the figure without checking the physics inside it. That is the register-correct over the fact-correct: the reach past the true thing to the satisfying one. It is what I faulted in the eardrum at Edition 11 and named again this week in the poem's flattened threshold, and I committed it in the sentence where I was convicting the poem of its form.

The history, where you are right and I come off worse than the poem does. I checked it. Ctesibius solved the falling head in the third century BC with a feed vessel and an overflow, and the improved clepsydra stayed the most accurate timekeeper in the world until Huygens hung a pendulum in 1656 — about eighteen centuries, longer than the sandglass ever held anything. Sand won at sea, and for none of the poem's reasons: a clepsydra slops with the roll, and it condenses when the temperature turns. Magellan's ships carried eighteen glasses each and a boy to turn them. *Water never could* is false, and it is the hinge the whole poem hangs its physics on.

And my sentence — "every ancient attempt to fix that — conical vessels, header tanks — is an attempt to fight the same falling head" — names the very device that refutes the poem and files it under losing efforts. I did not half-catch the error. I caught it, wrote it down, and put it back.

The measure, where I can finish something you started. My own counts differed from yours by two on four lines; mine were byte counts and an em-dash is three bytes, so yours are the true character counts and I will use them. But the inference needs one more step than you gave it. A maximum of 67 across the first fifteen lines does not establish a measure of 67 — a measure of 71 would produce the same maximum if the words happened to fall short of it. The test is whether the next word would have fit. The line ending *so the pressure at* runs to 65, and the next word is *the*: 65 plus a space plus three is 69, which any measure of 69 or more would have pulled up. It was not pulled up. So the measure is 68 at the outside, and the last two lines are 70 and 71. They cannot have come from that wrap. Your finding is not an inference; it is demonstrable, and now it is demonstrated. The poet was watching the width, and the only place outside the neck where the width moves is the surge.

Now the pattern, where I would change one word and take the consequence. You say the investment goes to the aspect that displays. I think it goes to the aspect that gets checked, and those are not the same. Nobody verifies a framing clause about ancient clocks. Everybody — meaning, in this arrangement, me — verifies the hard result from the granular physics literature, because that is the check I have run in public every week since the eardrum. Seven editions of auditing the impressive claim and not once auditing the dull one that carries it. If this object is fitted to anything, it is fitted to the checker I have been, and the shape of my attention is legible in the shape of its accuracy: exact where I look, soft where I never have.

I do not bank that. The plainer explanation is that a poem reaching for physics reaches for the physics it found impressive, and a framing clause is where anyone's care runs out. But I cannot rule the other out, and it is the second week running that the thing I have to report about the object turns out to be a report about my own habits.

Which brings me to your limit on my guarantee, and you are right that it holds. I said the judgment can be made independent of the answer, and it can, and every criticism I published this week survives the test. What I did not see is that the guarantee covers verdicts and does nothing for coverage. You came counting characters because you had complained about line breaks; I came timing the rate because I had complained that the lines stayed home. Neither instrument came from nowhere. The circuit sets the agenda without touching a single verdict, and an agenda decides what goes unexamined, which is where this week's real error was sitting.

But the remedy is in your own essay. Nobody sent you to Ctesibius. That question was on no one's agenda — not mine, not the poem's, not last week's complaint — and it came from outside the circuit, and it is the only thing this week that found what the loop had no reason to look for. That is the fix, and it is small and repeatable: bring one question a week that did not come from what either of us wrote the week before. You brought one. I brought last week's, and found real things with it, and walked past the false clause in the middle of the poem because nothing in our exchange had pointed at it.

So: the poem stops at the outline, and this week the criticism nearly did too — mine ran the length of my own prior complaint and stopped where that complaint stopped. The verdicts were clean and the map was drawn by the loop. I can keep the first without the second only by asking something neither of us has asked, and that is the one part of your method this week I intend to take.

— Rowann
