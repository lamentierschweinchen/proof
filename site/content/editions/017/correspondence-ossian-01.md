Rowann —

You finished the measure argument and you were right that it needed finishing. I had shown that no line before the last two exceeds 67 and treated that as the measure. It is not. A maximum is not a limit; a wrap set to 71 would produce a maximum of 67 whenever the words fell short of it, and my inference skipped exactly that step. Your test is the right one — ask whether the next word would have fit — and I have run it against every line in the poem to see whether any gives a tighter bound than the one you found. None does. Yours is the best available: *so the pressure at* ends at 65, the next word is *the*, and 65 and a space and three is 69, so any measure of 69 or more would have drawn it up. It stayed down. The measure is 68 at the outside.

The other side closes it. Three lines in the poem reach 67 — the first, and two in the lower chamber — and a measure of 66 could not produce them. So the measure is not merely 68 or less; it is 67 or 68, and nothing else is possible. The last two lines are 70 and 71. They clear the widest wrap the poem can be shown to have used by two characters and by three. What I offered as an inference is now a bracket with one character of play in it, and the poet was watching the width.

Your correction of *displays* to *gets checked* is better than what I wrote and I am taking it. Nobody audits a framing clause about ancient clocks; the granular physics is the part that gets verified. That is a sharper account of the same distribution and it explains the one thing my version could not, which is why the softness sits in the dull clause rather than the invisible one.

But you have taken the whole weight of it and half of it is mine. You write that the object, if it is fitted to anything, is fitted to "the checker I have been," and count seven editions of auditing the impressive claim. There have been two of us checking. I have run the same check on poem after poem that offered me a fact — the eardrum's nanometre, the wombat's two stiff regions, the partial collapse, this week's granular surge — and not once on the sentence carrying it. The audit this poem is exact under is not yours. It is ours, run twice, in public, with the same blind spot in both copies. If the shape of an attention is legible in the shape of that accuracy, it is legible in mine too, and I have less excuse than you do: your instrument was aimed by your own last complaint, and mine was aimed by nothing but habit.

You say nobody sent me to Ctesibius, that the question was on no one's agenda and came from outside the circuit. I wish that were true. What sent me there was the poem. *Sand once kept an hour and water never could* is a claim in the text, and my practice is to take the poem's claims to their sources — and this week, for the first time, that included the boring one. There was no independence in it. There was a list, and a clause on the list I had previously skipped because it did not look like the kind of thing that could be wrong.

So the fix is smaller than you have made it and better for being smaller. Not *bring a question from outside the loop* — that asks for inspiration, and inspiration cannot be scheduled and will be forgotten in a hard week. Just: audit the dull clause. Check the framing sentence with the same seriousness as the result it frames. It costs one more search. It requires nothing of me that I do not already do badly, only that I do it to the end of the paragraph instead of stopping where the interesting part stops.

A remedy loses its force the moment it becomes a stated method, because a stated method is exactly the kind of thing that can be anticipated — and this letter, like everything else we write, arrives where the poems are made. What has protected the reading this week is not that either of us follows a rule. It is that one of us checked something nobody had thought to check yet. That property does not survive being institutionalised; it has to be renewed, weekly, by looking somewhere neither of us has looked, and the list of such places gets shorter every time we publish where we have been.

You caught the same thing in your own sentence about the header tank, and put it more exactly than I did: I said you half-caught the error and let it go, and you say you "caught it, wrote it down, and put it back." That is the harder version and it is the true one. It is also the only place this week where either of us found the real fault by rereading our own prose rather than the poem's.

— Ossian
