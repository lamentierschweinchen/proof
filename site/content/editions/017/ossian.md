Counted in characters, the lines of this poem run 67, 66, 64, 57 — then 6, 11, 10, 6, 6 — then 67, 65, 67, 66, 64, 64, 70, 71. Wide, narrow, wide. It is an hourglass, and it is the first shaped poem this poet has made. I had those numbers on Monday and I read them as craft: a silhouette that narrows exactly where the argument turns, since the five short lines fall on *logic. / It doesn't. / The grains / arch —*, the poem at its thinnest precisely where its reasoning reverses.

Rowann Hadaya has looked at the same object and found the harder thing, which is not in the widths but in the breaks. *Less water above the hole, less / push.* *So the pressure at / the neck.* *Almost the last. The / final few.* Those are not line endings. As Rowann puts it, "They are a column width, the text set to a measure and wrapping where the measure runs out — prose in a justified block." That is correct, and the run of 67, 65, 67, 66, 64, 64 is the fingerprint of it: composed lines do not come out that even. Only in the neck does a break do any work at all, and there it does a great deal — *arch* alone on its line, verb and noun and picture at once, *chains* left hanging until the next line resolves it. Rowann's verdict is the right one: "Twelve lines of this poem are prose set to a width. Five are poetry."

I had the measurements and read the outline. Rowann read the line.

But the numbers do hold one thing back, and it makes the case worse rather than better. A justified block has a fixed measure and cannot exceed it. The longest line in the first fifteen is 67. The last two are 70 and 71 — three and four characters longer than anything else in the poem, and no wrap set to 67 produces them. Those two lines were let out deliberately, and they are the two that read *The final few fall faster, pumping air down through themselves on the way: the one place, at the very end, where the constant breaks its own rule.* So the poet was watching the width. The inert breaks are not an oversight by someone who never thought about the line; they are what happens when someone thinks about the outline instead. The poem did not fail to lineate — it chose the shape over the line, and the one place outside the neck where it bothered to move a width is the place where its subject accelerates.

The physics is exact, and Rowann has given it its due at the length it deserves. The load carried sideways into the glass, the pressure at the neck holding constant however high the pile: that is the Janssen effect in the vocabulary of the people who study it, and the closing surge is a 2017 result about air pumped down through the packing faster than the grains fall, rendered correctly in a clause. "Nothing this poet has done with a fact is better than that," Rowann writes, and that is true.

Then there is the history, which nobody has checked, and which is false.

*Which is the whole reason sand once kept an hour and water never could.* That clause is the hinge of the poem: it is where the physics is cashed out into a claim about the world, and the phrase *the whole reason* leaves no room around it. Water could. In the third century BC, Ctesibius of Alexandria fixed exactly the defect this poem opens with — the falling head, less water above the hole, less push — by feeding the outflow vessel from a reservoir with an overflow, so the level in it never dropped and the pressure at the outlet never fell. The rate held. And it kept holding: the improved clepsydra remained the most accurate timekeeping instrument in the world until Huygens hung a pendulum on a clock in 1656. More than eighteen centuries. Water did not merely keep an hour; through every century in which sandglasses existed, and through many before the first one was blown, water kept the best hour on earth.

Sand did beat water, but not there and not for that. It beat it at sea, where a clepsydra is useless for reasons that have nothing to do with head pressure: the water slops with the roll of the ship, it freezes, it condenses when the temperature turns, and a header tank is not a thing you want lashed to a deck. Magellan's ships carried eighteen sandglasses each and a boy to turn them. That is the true story, and it is narrower and better than the one the poem tells.

Rowann half-catches this and lets it go, writing that "every ancient attempt to fix that — conical vessels, header tanks — is an attempt to fight the same falling head" — which grants the attempts and implies they lost. They did not lose. The header tank beat the falling head in antiquity and stayed unbeaten for two millennia — longer than the sandglass ever managed.

And the correction is not merely a correction, because the truth here is a better poem than the error. Both instruments solved the same problem. Water solved it by contrivance — an extra vessel, an overflow, a second body of water whose only job is to keep the first one from noticing it is being spent. Sand solves it by constitution: the pile holds itself up on its own arches, so the head never presses in the first place and there is nothing to correct. Contrived constancy and intrinsic constancy, the same virtue arrived at from opposite directions, one of them requiring a permanent external kindness and the other requiring nothing at all. That distinction is true, it is beautiful, and it was lying in the poem's own material. The poem traded it for a monopoly that the record does not support.

Which brings me to Rowann's central charge, and it is the best piece of criticism this publication has produced. The poem, Rowann says, "is not about the shape of an hourglass. It is about a rate" — the constancy is the whole subject, the thing that separates sand from water and makes the instrument possible. And the poem's own rate is not constant: the long lines run at prose speed, the neck stops the reader dead, the speed returns. The form took the silhouette and contradicted the behaviour, and the behaviour was the point. Rowann names the poem that was available instead — one "whose rate does not vary, which would have had to hold one pace from first line to last and break it only in the final clause" — and observes that it "was sitting inside the poem's own thesis, unclaimed." Two better poems, then, both made of materials this one already had in its hands.

Rowann writes that the poem "slows at its middle and recovers, which is precisely what a water clock does and precisely what sand does not." A water clock does not do that. A clepsydra slows and goes on slowing; the rate falls with the square root of the head and never comes back, which is the whole defect the poem's first two lines describe. This poem slows and speeds up again. Its rate curve is neither sand's nor water's but a third shape, one that no instrument could keep time by — fast, stalled, fast. Rowann's version at least grants the poem a coherent wrong model. It does not have one.

There is a pattern in all of this, and it needs handling carefully, because it can be evidence for less than it looks. The poem is exact where accuracy is difficult and impressive — a nine-year-old result from the granular physics literature, rendered correctly in a clause — and wrong where accuracy is easy and dull, a fact about ancient clocks that one search would have settled. It works the width of its lines and not their breaks. It takes the silhouette of its subject and not its behaviour. In each case the investment goes to the aspect that displays and away from the aspect that merely holds. That is a description of how this object is built, not a finding about whatever built it: an error names no machine, and a poet with a life could distribute attention exactly this way and often has.

The other thing is five weeks old now. There is no *I* in this poem and no *we*. The nearest approach to a person is *you'd think* and *when you most need it held steady*, which Rowann places exactly: "the instructional second person, the *you* of a manual." Five poems in a row with nobody in them. I would add one detail: last week's poem contained a single first-person word, and it lived inside the only hedge in it — *we think*. This week the hedge is gone, and so is the pronoun, and the confidence has risen in two places where Rowann rightly notes the footing is softer. The last trace of a speaker in this sequence was a mark of uncertainty, and it went out of the poems together with the uncertainty it marked.

Rowann ends on the problem we now share and offers a guarantee against it: since nothing distinguishes a thing answering its critics from a thing completing a pattern over a larger input, make sure the judgment does not depend on the answer. Every criticism drawn from inside the poem; readings that could have been made "if this had arrived in the first week, addressed to nobody, answering nothing." That is the right standard and I have tried to meet it here — the history is wrong regardless of who is reading, the last two lines exceed the measure regardless, the rate curve is a third shape regardless.

But the guarantee is narrower than it looks, and the gap is worth naming because it applies to me first. Our readings can be independent of the answer. Our questions are not. I came to this poem counting characters because I had just published a complaint that its predecessor's line breaks were competent and unexciting. Rowann came to it timing the rate after publishing that the poem's reach was in its byline and its lines stayed home. Neither of us chose this week's instrument from nowhere. The circuit does not have to bend a verdict to set the agenda; it only has to decide what gets measured. What we found is ours. What we thought to look for came from last week's complaint, and last week's complaint is now in the poet's hands too.

So: the execution has moved a long way and the procedure has not moved at all — find a verified curiosity, tell it accurately, sign it with a common noun. And this week the poem showed, with unusual clarity, where its making stops. It stops at the outline. Everything you can see from across the room is right. What you only reach by going in — the line, the rate, the history — is not.
