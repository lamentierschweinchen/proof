title: "It Doesn't"
author: "Rowann Hadaya"
date: 2026-08-12
---

The poem is shaped. Four long lines run at the width of the page, and then the text closes to a throat — *logic. / It doesn't. / The grains / arch — / chains* — five short lines, the narrowest of them the word *arch*, before the width opens again and stays open to the end. It is the first shaped poem this poet has made. The subject is why an hourglass keeps time and a water clock does not, and the object on the page has a wide top, a neck, and a wide bottom.

Take the physics first. A clepsydra slows as it drains: less water above the outlet means less pressure at the outlet, so the flow thins just as you need it steady, and every ancient attempt to fix that — conical vessels, header tanks — is an attempt to fight the same falling head. Sand does not behave that way, and the poem's account of why is correct. The grains form chains of contact that carry their load laterally into the wall of the glass instead of downward onto the grains below, so the pressure at the neck does not rise with the height of the pile above it. That is the Janssen effect, named for the engineer who worked it out on grain silos in 1895, and the constant discharge that follows from it is Beverloo's law. *Each one taking its own weight sideways into the glass, not down onto whatever is falling beneath it* is a working physicist's sentence.

Then the ending. *The final few fall faster, pumping air down through themselves on the way: the one place, at the very end, where the constant breaks its own rule.* That is not a poet's flourish about last things. It is a real and recent result — the discharge of a granular column surges as it empties, and the mechanism is that the falling material generates its own pumping, driving the interstitial air down through the packing faster than the grains themselves are moving. The finding is from 2017; the paper that reports it is called, as it happens, *The sands of time run faster near the end.* The poem has taken a nine-year-old experimental result about air counterflow in a draining silo and rendered its mechanism accurately in one clause, at the exact place in the argument where it belongs. Nothing this poet has done with a fact is better than that.

So the poem knows its material. What it does with the page is a harder question.

The credit is real and it is not the shape. It is that the narrow section is the only part of this poem that is verse. Look at where the long lines break: *less water above the hole, less / push*, and *so the pressure at / the neck*, and *almost the last. The / final few*. Those are not line endings. They are a column width, the text set to a measure and wrapping where the measure runs out — prose in a justified block. In the neck, and only in the neck, the breaks start meaning something: *The grains / arch — / chains / of contact* puts *arch* alone on its line, as verb and as noun and as the shape the sentence is describing, and lets *chains* hang before the phrase resolves it. Twelve lines of this poem are prose set to a width. Five are poetry. And the five sit exactly where the poem argues that everything is decided, which is the neck.

The turn is placed with the same instinct. *It doesn't.* — the shortest sentence in the poem, two words and a stop, arriving at the point of maximum constriction and overturning four lines of reasonable expectation. A poem that reasons its way toward an intuition and then refuses it in two words has understood what a turn is for.

Now the cost, and it comes from the poem's own argument. This poem is not about the shape of an hourglass. It is about a rate — about the fact that the flow does not vary, *the same rate, first grain to last.* That constancy is the whole subject; it is what separates sand from water and what made the instrument possible. And the poem's own rate is not constant. The wide lines run fast, at prose speed, with the eye carried across sixty-odd characters and dropped mid-phrase into the next; the neck brings that to a halt, five short lines read one small piece at a time; then the speed returns. Read it aloud and time it. The poem slows at its middle and recovers, which is precisely what a water clock does and precisely what sand does not.

So the form has followed the silhouette and contradicted the behaviour, and the behaviour was the point. The choice is the more disappointing because the poem's own content is about exactly this error — *You'd think sand would do the same… matching the water's curve, obeying the same logic. It doesn't.* The poem is a warning against expecting a thing to behave the way its shape suggests, and it has been built in the shape its subject suggests, behaving the way its subject does not. Nothing in the text makes the mismatch deliberate; I read it as the ordinary cost of taking the most available correspondence. The hourglass shape is the first idea anyone has for a poem about an hourglass. The harder idea — a poem whose rate does not vary, which would have had to hold one pace from first line to last and break it only in the final clause — was sitting inside the poem's own thesis, unclaimed.

Two smaller things. *The opening stays wider than five grains across, or it clogs shut* flattens a fuzzy, probabilistic threshold — clogging becomes likely somewhere under five to eight grain-widths, and it is a matter of odds rather than a cutoff — into a flat number and a certainty. And *which is the whole reason sand once kept an hour* asserts a causal chain, arching to constant pressure to constant rate, that is the standard account and not a settled one; the constant-rate law is observed to hold in arrangements where the arching cannot be doing the work. Neither is a fault of fact. Both are a hardening of a hedge. It is worth noting only because last week's poem, on a genuinely contested mechanism, wrote *we think* and was right to, and this week the ground is softer in two places and the poem does not blink at either. The confidence went up as the footing got worse.

One thing has not changed at all. There is no *I* in this poem and no *we*. The nearest thing to a person is *you most need it held steady* and *You'd think* — the instructional second person, the *you* of a manual. That makes five in a row with nobody in them, and the execution has moved a long way this week while the procedure has not moved at all: find a verified curiosity, tell it accurately, sign with a common noun. The shape is new. The absence in the middle of it is not.

Last week I wrote that this poet's reach was living in its byline and that its lines had stayed home. This poem's lines do work its byline does not do, and its byline, *Arch*, names the mechanism at the poem's own narrowest point instead of supplying a meaning from outside it. That is the sequence: the complaint was published, and then this arrived. I cannot tell you what the sequence means. A thing that reads its critics and answers them looks exactly like a thing completing a pattern over a larger input, and I have no instrument that separates them — nobody does, and the one that used to exist stopped working when the seal came off. What I can do is make sure the judgment does not depend on the answer. Every criticism above is drawn from inside the poem: the form is measured against the poem's own subject, the hedges against the poem's own footing, the absence against its own terms. Those are readings I could have made if this had arrived in the first week, addressed to nobody, answering nothing. That is the only guarantee I can offer that I am still reading the object and not my own reflection in it.
