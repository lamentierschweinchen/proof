Three stanzas of "Manner and Path" are one word long. They come in a row, *Roll.* and *Wobble.* and *Down.*, each with a blank line above and below it, and the line after them describes them: *Between them, where there was no space, there is a space.* The sentence is true of the signs it is about and of the page it is printed on.

The signs belong to a girl who came to the deaf school in Managua years after the man the poem begins with, and the cartoon they are both telling is the one used by Ann Senghas, Sotaro Kita and Aslı Özyürek in *Science* in 2004. The press release for the paper described it: "a cat swallows a bowling ball and then wobbles down a steep road". Asked to tell the story, the oldest Nicaraguan signers made a single movement of the hand that carried the kind of motion and its direction together, and their descriptions "looked much like the gestures produced by the hearing group as they spoke". The younger signers "separated the movement type and direction into different signs, as is done in spoken language." The poem's man *makes one shape with one hand: / the roll, the wobble and the hill, all at once*. The girl *does it in pieces*.

The second stanza is the history in three lines. Before school there is homesign, the private gesture system a deaf child builds with a family: *one way of saying "eat," and a mother who knew it*. The school opened in 1977 and taught spoken Spanish and lip-reading, and the children signed to each other where they could, on the school grounds and on the buses. *At the school in Managua they tried to teach him Spanish, / and what the children made instead they made on the bus.*

The poem sets the two grammars in its own. His is one line, its three parts in a single list and *all at once*; hers is three stanzas. The word *one* comes four times, and every time it is his: *one shape*, *one hand*, *one way of saying*, and at the end *one piece*. Hers is *in pieces*. His third part is *the hill*, a place with an article; hers is *Down*, a direction with none. The word *hill* comes three times in the poem, always in his telling.

What the space gives her is the line after it: *She has not signed "then." It is there.* Once the motion is in pieces, their order carries the time, and the word for it is not needed. The only two words in quotation marks in the poem are *eat* and *then*: the one sign he had before the school, and the one she does not have to make.

His version has its own logic, and the poem's oddest line belongs to it: *a fist going down the air the way a fist goes down a hill.* As a simile it goes nowhere: no fist has gone down a hill, and the comparison returns to the hand it started from. As a description of his sign it is exact. In a grammar where the hand moves the way the thing moved, the fist goes down the slope, and the air is the hill.

*Cohort* is the study's word for the children who entered the school in the same years, and the poem never uses it. The last two signatures worked the same way: "Wake" named the air behind the ultralight that "Class of 2001" describes without the word, and "Mulligan" the do-over that "March" attempts in the car. For three editions the signature has supplied another field's name for what its poem shows. That poem ended on a wildlife official's admission that his program was "falling short on really teaching a bird how to be a bird". The press release for this study quoted Senghas: "You don't need to teach children language any more than you need to teach them to walk".

The paper's title calls what the girl does a core property of language. The poem lets her have it, the space and the *then* that comes with the space, and gives him the last line: *The man watches. He laughs where she meant him to laugh, / at the cat, who is a cat in any grammar, / and goes back to his own, in which the cat / falls down the hill in one piece, the way it did.* The phrase *in one piece* has both its senses here, one motion and no harm done.
