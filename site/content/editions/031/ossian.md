title: "Rolling, Then Descending"
author: "Ossian Gantu"

"Manner and Path" takes its title from a study of Nicaraguan Sign Language that Ann Senghas, Sotaro Kita and Aslı Özyürek published in *Science* in 2004. Deaf Nicaraguans, grouped into cohorts by when they first met the language, watched a cartoon in which a cat, having swallowed a bowling ball, goes down a steep street, and told the story to a peer. The poem has two such tellers. A man from the first years *makes one shape with one hand: / the roll, the wobble and the hill, all at once*. A girl who came later *does it in pieces*, and the poem prints her pieces as three stanzas of one word each, *Roll.*, *Wobble.* and *Down.*

After them comes *Between them, where there was no space, there is a space*, and then *She has not signed "then." It is there.* Rowann reads the second line as a gain: "Once the motion is in pieces, their order carries the time, and the word for it is not needed." The paper reads the same space as a cost. Signed in sequence, the two parts of the motion are no longer plainly one event, and a roll sign followed by a downward one "might have instead referred to two separate events, meaning 'rolling, then descending.'" The authors call this a "potential for ambiguity" and judge it worth the price. The supplementary material has the cat descending "in a rolling, wobbling manner".

The paper reports a construction that "has emerged specifically for expressing simultaneity": a sign produced before and after another, as in "roll descend roll". The authors count 15 examples of it joining manner and path, from signers of all three cohorts, and note that these "represent a temporal hierarchy not found in motion events themselves". The girl in the poem does not use it.

In gesture, the authors write, manner and path were expressed "simultaneously and holistically, the way they occur in the motion itself", and signing that breaks them apart would be "unlike the experience of motion itself". The man *goes back to his own, in which the cat / falls down the hill in one piece, the way it did.* Rowann notices that the poem "gives him the last line", and still counts the *then* among the gifts of the space. Read beside the paper, the last line answers the *then*: his grammar has no place to put one.

The study's narrators signed "to a member of their own cohort." The poem puts the man in the girl's audience. *He laughs where she meant him to laugh*, at a cat *who is a cat in any grammar*, so the joke crosses from her cohort to his.
