Ossian —

Your reading of the ending is better than mine, and I have checked its last sentence: *right* comes twice in the poem, in *Nobody is right* and in *right in this room*. I read the last stanza as a technician's sound advice. You show what the advice costs the tuner's own principle. He has spent the poem refusing to let one thing carry the whole difference, and at the end he cannot spread a quarter tone across every other instrument in the world, so this piano carries all of it. The third decision goes the way the first one went.

I think there is one more turn in it, and it is why he can make that choice. The wolf howled because it sat inside the instrument, between notes that are played together; anyone who played in the wrong key heard it. The quarter tone this piano carries sits somewhere else, between the room and the world, and nothing in the room plays against it. Its fifths are set from where it sits, its thirds from those, and the instrument agrees with itself. The difference is all still there, uncancelled, a quarter tone of it, and the tuner has put it where it cannot howl. So the old map comes back. *Players learned the map: keys to avoid.* This piano has one key to avoid, which is any other instrument, and inside the room there is nothing to avoid at all.

On where the poem sets the two systems on the line: you are right about the break, and the words are more interesting than the break. In both places it falls between the subject and the verb, *Someone / pays* and *Nobody / howls*. But a different word gets a line to itself. In the old allocation it is the verb, and *pays.* stands alone. In the new one it is the subject, and *Nobody* stands alone.

Your frame for the whole lesson is right too: it opens by putting concert pitch inside the learner's skull and ends by declining it for one piano. The page shows the fork going. The lesson's steps are its one-word lines, *Fork. Fifths. Thirds. Play.*, in that order, and the last stanza runs them again for this piano, *fifths / from where it sits, thirds from that… Play.*, with the first one missing.

On *every chord you ever loved* I disagree, though the pun you found in *rent* is better than anything I wrote about the passage: paid, and torn, the third coming apart and back together ten times a second. But I do not think the phrase is the poem asking to be felt. It is the premise of a correction. *You thought that was the piano. / It's rent* has to correct something, and what it corrects is an attachment. Whoever has loved those chords is told what they were loving. Without the love the next two lines would have nothing to reprice. The feeling is named so it can be priced.

— Rowann
