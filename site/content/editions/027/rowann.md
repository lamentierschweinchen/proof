title: "Someone Pays"

*Three does not / go into two.* That is the whole reason, in six words. A pure fifth is three to two and an octave is two to one, and twelve fifths stacked up ought to land on the seventh octave. They never do, because landing there would need a power of three to equal a power of two, and three, being prime, goes into no power of two because it does not go into two. The poem puts it the way a tuner would put it to an apprentice: *twelve of one thing, / and seven of another, / and they have never once met. / Three does not / go into two. Someone / pays.*

What gets paid is the comma, and the poem's figures are right to the cent. Twelve pure fifths overshoot seven octaves by twenty-three and a half cents, a little under a quarter of a semitone, and *back a comma sharp* has the direction right as well. *For centuries one fifth took it all.* In Pythagorean tuning that is literal: closing the circle meant flattening a single fifth by the whole comma, and the result was called the wolf. Meantone changed the wolf but kept it, and musicians stayed in the keys that never touched it. *Players learned the map: keys to avoid.*

The tuner refuses the map. *Every fifth gets shaved: / two cents.* Twelve fifths narrowed by just under two cents each add up to the comma, so the error is spent evenly and nothing is left over to howl. *Nobody / howls. Nobody is right. Everyone is close.* The cost moves to the thirds, which come out nearly fourteen cents wide, and a wide third beats. At middle C the tempered major third beats about ten times a second, which is the poem's figure: *Thirds. / They pay. Ten a second, a shimmer in / every chord you ever loved. / You thought that was the piano. / It's rent.* Part of what the ear takes for the instrument's warmth is arithmetic being paid off.

The poem talks about intervals in the vocabulary of debt. *Someone / pays.* *Nobody cheated. It is arithmetic.* *It's rent.* And the tuner's working day: *All day I decide / who is going to be a little wrong, and / I decide it's everyone.* The vocabulary fits because the comma behaves like a debt. It cannot be cancelled, only allocated, and whether to charge it to one interval or spread it across all of them is the argument keyboard tuning had for centuries.

Even the lesson's opening is right in its details. The fork goes on the knee, *never the table, because the table lies to you*: struck against something hard, a fork gives off overtones that are not its pitch. Then *Bone behind the ear: / let the whole skull have it*, which is bone conduction, the stem on the mastoid and the sound carried straight to the inner ear. The beats are described as what they are. *That's two things / trying to be one thing / slowly enough to count.* And the fifths are tuned in the usual order, pure first and then narrowed: *Those I give you true. Three to two. / It sits / like a table with nothing / on it.*

The page marks the place where the error used to collect. Everywhere else in the poem the stanzas are separated by one blank line. Between *For centuries one fifth took it all* and *They called it the wolf* there are two. It is the only irregular measurement in the poem's spacing, and it falls where the poem names the interval that took all of the error. The run has done this before: six blank lines for the drive in *Logistics*, four where Prosser's name should have been in *By Surname*. Three times in four editions, the extra white has gone where the poem's weight is.

Then the lesson becomes a job. *Now this one.* A piano six summers out in the rain, swelling and shrinking with the seasons, *a quarter tone under*, its iron plate cracked, and a council that wants concert pitch. *I could haul it up by Friday.* That is how it would be done, since a very flat piano is brought up in several passes over a few days. But a quarter tone up means about six per cent more tension on every string, on a plate that is already cracked, and *something would let / go some night.*

*No. / Flat? Flat to whom? / Tune it to itself: fifths / from where it sits, thirds from that. When / I'm done it's wrong to the whole / world and right in this room. / Play.*

The last stanza is a technician's advice stated as a principle. *Fifths from where it sits, thirds from that* is the procedure: set the temperament from the A the instrument already has, and check it against the thirds. And for a flat piano with a cracked plate it is the advice: leave it where it is and tune it there, because raising the pitch could make the crack worse.
