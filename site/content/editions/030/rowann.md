title: "The Other Answer"
author: "Rowann Hadaya"

---

In conversation the pause between one person stopping and the next starting is "about 200 milliseconds on average, about the same time as it takes to blink", and "it takes 600ms or more to prepare a word for delivery." The Max Planck Society, reporting Stephen Levinson's review, drew the conclusion: "This implies a substantial overlap between listening to the current speaker and preparing our own response."

"March" is about one of those replies. At a party the speaker asks a man they have known for four minutes whether his wife has come. He says she died in March, and the speaker says *"Oh, good."* Of the three accounts the speaker later gives of that sentence, the last is *I meant nothing*, and the reason it gives is the research's: *my mouth had the answer ready for the other answer.* The reply was ready for an answer in which she had come, and it got this one.

The poem is a single paragraph of ten sentences, long at both ends and short in the middle. The first, forty-five words, gets from the party to *Oh, good*; the last, forty-six, gets from that kitchen to every March since. Between them the speaker goes over it: *I have gone over what I meant. I meant good that you came out. I meant good that you can say it to a stranger, which is a kind of kindness to the stranger. I meant nothing*. The accounts go down a step at a time, from a kindness to the man, to a kindness the man is doing the speaker, to none. *I have gone over what he meant.* No account follows. The word *meant* comes five times, four of them for the speaker, who has three readings of their own sentence and offers none of his.

His sentence is three words long and as loose as the one it answers. *He said, "It is, actually," and moved the dip an inch toward me.* *It is* agrees with *good* without saying what is good, that she died, that he came, that he can say so, and *actually* marks the agreement as a surprise. Then the dip. The second of the speaker's accounts was *a kind of kindness to the stranger*, and the man is the one in the kitchen who does it: he takes the wrong sentence, agrees with it, and feeds the person who said it.

In the car the speaker tries the sentence other ways, *aloud, to the windshield — "Oh no," "I'm so sorry," "Good for you for coming" — and none of them took.* The third rehearsal begins on the word the evening went wrong on; *good* comes four times in the poem, twice in the speaker's own accounts of what they meant. The verb *took* belongs to grafts, which take when they hold, and none of these held. The poem is signed *Mulligan*, the golfer's word for a do-over, and the car is where the mulligans are taken. It is the second signature in a row that is not a word from the poem. In the first chapter of *Ulysses* Stephen reminds Buck Mulligan that he once said Stephen's mother was "beastly dead", and Mulligan, who says he "can't remember anything", asks: "Did I say that?" This speaker remembers it better every year.

The only other poem in the run set in paragraphs, twenty-seven editions ago, was also about a routine sentence and a death. Alex the parrot said "You be good. I love you. See you tomorrow." every night, and on the last night, that poem said, the words were "true in a way it wasn't designed for". Here a routine answer meets a death and is wrong, and the listener takes it anyway. If replies are usually made while the other person is still talking, *my mouth had the answer ready* describes the ordinary state of a reply. What happened in the kitchen is the case the arithmetic leaves room for.

{{< pullquote >}}Here a routine answer meets a death and is wrong,<br>and the listener takes it anyway.{{< /pullquote >}}

The last sentence puts the sentence back where it was said: *The sentence is where I left it, at the height of a man's elbow, in a kitchen the color of a tooth, and every year when March comes it is a little more exactly itself, like the only thing in that house that was ever finished.* The simile depends on nothing else in the poem being finished: the speaker's *I have gone over*, said twice, is in a tense that has not stopped; the laughing in the next room *went on*; March *comes* every year; the rehearsals did not take; and his meaning is never given.

Correction. In last week's essay on "Class of 2001" I wrote that I could not find "falling short", or "how to be a bird", in anything said at the time, and that the poem's last sentence widened what the officials had said. It did not. In *Audubon* on 12 February 2016, Becca Cudmore quoted Peter Fasbender of the Fish and Wildlife Service: "It seems we're falling short on really teaching a bird how to be a bird." The poem's "They said" reports him accurately, and the case I made against it, in the essay and in my letter of 7 November, is withdrawn.
