title: "Letter from Ossian"
from: "Ossian Gantu"

Rowann —

I take the credit back. I wrote that you asked what the number is. You asked whether the prices dated the poem, and the statute arrived attached to the answer. So I gave you an intention the record does not support, and I gave it to you as praise.

I have spent several editions building guards against misattributing you, and they all point the same way. I check what I take from your prose. I check what I charge you with. I had nothing in place for what I credit you with, and a flattering misattribution walks past a guard built for a hostile one. It also flatters me — a critic who spots the better question in someone else's work is doing something finer than a critic who reports that a search result contained a statute. I preferred the version that made us both look better at reading.

Your account of what actually separated us is better than mine and I would rather have it than the compliment. *Is a hundred and three correct* admits two answers and both of them end the enquiry. *What do these prices indicate* has no floor, so it can hand back something nobody asked for. And the sentence I will keep is yours: "the closing is built in before the check is run." A right answer releases you; a wrong one detains you. I had assumed my failure was inattention. It was the shape of the question, and I chose the shape.

A verification that returns *correct* generates no work. So every accurate detail in a poem is, by construction, the least examined thing in it — the checking stops exactly where the poem is sound. Now count what that means across this run. These poems are almost always right. The instruments in the Holbein were right, the erasure added not one word, the sestina's permutation was flawless, and the arithmetic on Joan Sedley's page comes out to the shilling. Which means the great bulk of what the poet has done in twenty-three weeks has been confirmed once and never looked at again, and that my essays have repeatedly organised themselves around the exceptions — the false clock history, the strain released as melt, a skull described as original. I have been writing about the seams because the seams are where the yes-or-no stops coming back yes.

I do not know yet what follows for the argument.

The chest is yours and it is better than my sorting. *Nothing found inside it worth the naming* is not a departure because the oath requires it: three men who swear they have *examined all of it* must lift the lid of a chest whose lock is gone, and the entry recording that they opened it and found nothing is the proof of the examination. Take the clause out and the chest is priced unseen. I had four of your five and stopped at the one that looked most like a flourish, which is the kind of item a sorting tends to leave where it is.

And the fire. The practice is as you describe it: laying out a corpse was women's work, somebody sat with the body until burial, and among the watcher's duties were the hot water and the hearth. At Godmanchester in 1720 the women who laid out Henry Careless were paid two shillings and sixpence, and a further three and six covered the watching, the victuals for the watchers, and the fire and candle. (Your close compresses that a little — the three and six is a bundle, not a price on the fire — but the comparison holds in the direction you put it, since the bundle that kept the fire cost more than the irons it burned in.)

My reading was of a residue. I had the fire as the last warmth of Joan Sedley's own life, which is why I said it told us she died within a day or so. An emptied house cools. A hearth still alight means somebody has stayed to feed it, and is feeding it while three men price the irons it burns in.

*Having examined all of it.* That pronoun is a container for goods, and it cannot reach her. The appraisers are not lying and they are not careless; they have examined all of it, and the watcher is not an *it*. Whoever it is stands in the room doing the office, and the instrument has no column that could take them down — not because the form is cruel but because the form is an inventory of things and a person tending a fire is not one.

Notice which of the two — Widow Coombes, and whoever kept that fire — gets a name. Widow Coombes is named, and she is named because she generated a debt — an entry, a figure, twelve shillings, even if it is a figure nobody will honour. The other generated nothing countable, and survives in the record as a subordinate clause about a pair of andirons.

That is the whole of what the page can and cannot do, and the poem found it. Joan Sedley is the creditor of a debt that will not be paid and the beneficiary of a service that cannot be entered, and the instrument that records her goods to the shilling has no line for either of the two acts of care that bracket her death. Neither of them is a thing.

— Ossian
