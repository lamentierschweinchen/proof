Joan Sedley's goods come to five pounds and three shillings. Since the Act of 1529, an inventory of a dead person's goods and chattels was required where those goods exceeded five pounds. Her estate clears that line by three shillings. Value the cow a little lower, or leave the twenty shillings Richard Hale owes her off the page, and there is no document at all — no three neighbours walking the rooms, no list, nothing of her on paper but a burial.

Eight shillings in the hall, twenty-two in the chamber, fifty-three in the yard: eighty-three shillings of goods. Add Hale's twenty and the total is a hundred and three, which is five pounds and three shillings, which is what the poem says. The arithmetic holds. What the sum does not contain is the last entry on the page: twelve shillings owed her by the parish for nursing the Widow Coombes through her last sickness, filed as *supposed desperate* — the period's term for a debt nobody expects to see again.

The genre is handled by someone who knows it. Appraisers were two or three local men, usually neighbours, and *indifferent* here is not indifference but its older sense, impartial, the quality the job requires. *Sperate* and *desperate* are the real categories: recoverable and not. A flock bed is the poor relation of a feather one. *Sad-coloured* wool is a trap for the modern ear — it means sober, deliberately subdued, and not sorrowful, and the poem uses it correctly while letting the other reading sit there. Six hives *empty since Michaelmas*: a skep-keeper killed the colony over a sulphur pit to get the honey out, though the taking-up is usually dated to St Bartholomew's in August. Michaelmas is the quarter day, when rents fell due and accounts were reckoned, which is the clock a document like this one keeps. The room order, the andirons, the form, the valuation in shillings — all of it is right.

Joan Sedley was a healer. She owned *a book for the curing of agues and the setting of bones, in her own hand*, and it is appraised at one shilling, which is what a broken chest with nothing in it is also worth. The other book is a Bible with *her name written in the front in a hand not her own*. So: the book she was given, inscribed by somebody else, two shillings; the book she made, one. And the twelve shillings at the bottom of the page are what the parish owes her for doing the thing the one-shilling book is about.

That is the whole argument of the poem and it is made entirely in numbers. Her vocation cannot be valued by this procedure. As an object it is worth a shilling. As labour it is worth twelve shillings that will not be paid.

The appraisal can price a cow at fifty shillings — three-fifths of everything she owned — and can do nothing whatever with the fact that she sat up with a dying woman.

The twelve shillings appear after the attestation. *We set our hands to this, being present, and having examined all of it, and found nothing we have cause to doubt* — and then, below the signatures, the desperate debt. In a real inventory the appraisers' subscription closes the document; the order runs goods, debts, total, names. I could not establish whether desperate debts were routinely counted in the sum or kept out of it. The position, though, is not in question. Whatever a real inventory did with such a debt, it did it above the signatures. This one is printed after three men have sworn they examined everything, which means the thing they did not examine is the thing she did for someone else.

*Nothing found inside it worth the naming.* *The fire not out when they came in.* *Giving less than she once did.* *Carded and not spun.* And, of the medical book, *in her own hand this time.* No appraiser writes *this time*. It is a narrator's aside, two words long, pointing back at the Bible to make sure the reader has caught the contrast. The same is true of the fire and the cow. These are the best lines on the page and every one of them is a place where the document stops being a document.

An inventory's authority is its disinterest. That is the entire basis of the form: three men with no stake, sworn *indifferent*, writing down what is in front of them at what it would fetch. The poem wants that authority — the flatness, the officialdom, the way a valuation in shillings can say more about a life than a description of it — and then reserves the right to arrange. Every departure from the clerk's voice lands on an emotional beat. Nothing is left where it fell.

A pastiche is allowed to invent, and the objection is not that Joan Sedley never lived. It is that the form's power comes from nobody having composed it, and this one is composed throughout — the wool stopped between carding and spinning, the fire still warm, the cow declining on schedule, the healer's book priced at a shilling. A genuine inventory is moving by accident: the objects fall where a life left them, and the pathos is the reader's work.

Here the objects have been arranged, by a voice that swears it has no interest in the outcome.

The last word on the page is the signature, *Supposed*, taken out of *supposed sperate* and *supposed desperate* — the appraisers' guess about which debts are real. It is the right word for what the document does. Everything here is a supposition: what the goods would fetch, which debts will come in, what a life was worth in shillings.
