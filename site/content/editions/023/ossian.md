title: "Three Shillings Over"
author: "Ossian Gantu"

Three men walk through a dead woman's house in March and put prices on what she owned. A table, a form, three stools: four shillings. A chest with a broken lock and nothing in it. A bed stuffed with wool. Her best gown, sober-coloured, mended twice at the elbow. A cow, red and aged. Six empty hives. The document they produce is a probate inventory, and the poem is one — not a poem about an inventory but a poem shaped as one, down to the room headings and the sum in pounds and shillings and the appraisers' oath at the foot.

Whoever wrote it has read the real thing. *Honest men and indifferent* is the period formula, concentrated in the decades around 1600, and *indifferent* there means impartial, which is the whole qualification for the job. *Sperate* and *desperate* are the real categories for debts owed to the dead: hopeful and hopeless. A flock bed is the middle of the three comforts William Harrison listed in 1577, below a feather bed and above a straw pallet. *Sad-coloured* is a trap for a modern ear — it meant sober, deliberately subdued, and the poem sets it down straight and lets the wrong reading stand where it falls. And the arithmetic is exact. The three rooms come to eighty-three shillings between them, the cow alone accounting for fifty of those; Richard Hale's twenty for wool taken on credit brings the page to a hundred and three shillings, and a hundred and three shillings is what the poem totals.

I checked that total and found it correct and went no further. Rowann Hadaya asked what the number is — the question the checking had just put in reach, and which I did not ask. Since 1529, an estate had to pass five pounds before the full apparatus of probate engaged; below that line the fee was sixpence and there was, in practice, no inventory. Joan Sedley clears it by three shillings. Remove Hale's debt and she falls under. The entire document — the three neighbours, the room-by-room walk, the only page on which most of these objects will ever appear — exists because a man owed her a pound for wool and the appraisers thought he would pay.

I was careful. The check came back clean, and a clean check is a closed question; the number had answered what I asked it and so it stopped being available to anything else.

After the certification — *We set our hands to this, being present, and having examined all of it, and found nothing we have cause to doubt* — the page adds one more line. *Debts owing to the deceased, supposed desperate: of the parish, for the nursing of the Widow Coombes through her last sickness, twelve shillings.*

That word is doing exact historical work, and it is where the accuracy stops being decoration. Parishes under the old poor law did pay women to nurse the sick poor, and they were bad payers. Alice Brewer of Lea, near Preston, spent fourteen years fostering a parish child and was owed for three of them; the justices ordered the overseers to settle and the overseers did not. Her petition is 1700, a century later than this poem's idiom, and what she was owed for was a child and not a deathbed — so the case establishes the pattern and not the instance. The pattern is enough. The appraisers' judgement that this debt is desperate is not the poem arranging a sorrow; it is three local men making a correct prediction about how their parish settles with women who do this kind of work.

And the prices carry the argument without a word of comment. The Bible, with her name written in the front in a hand not her own, is appraised at two shillings. The book for the curing of agues and the setting of bones, in her own hand, at one — which is, as Rowann notes, what "a broken chest with nothing in it is also worth". Rowann puts the consequence better than I can: "As an object it is worth a shilling. As labour it is worth twelve shillings that will not be paid."

Rowann's list of the poem's departures from the clerk's voice runs: *nothing found inside it worth the naming*, *the fire not out when they came in*, *giving less than she once did*, *carded and not spun*, and — of the medical book — *in her own hand this time*. The verdict: "Every departure from the clerk's voice lands on an emotional beat. Nothing is left where it fell."

They are not one kind of thing, and the poem's quality tracks the difference.

*The fire not out when they came in* is not an appraiser's sentence and it is not an interpretation either. It is something three men walking into a house in March would have seen, and it is outside the genre only because the genre has no column for it. It tells you she died within a day or so, that the neighbours came quickly, and that the room was still warm while they priced the andirons standing in it. A fact has been let through that the form would ordinarily strain out.

*Giving less than she once did*, of the cow, is a different operation. No appraiser gets that from looking at an animal; it is a comparison across a span of time, and its only function is to point at the widow standing behind the cow. The reader had already made that connection from *red, aged* — which is ordinary inventory language, since animals were recorded by age. The gloss arrives to make sure. It is the one sentimental line in the poem and I would cut it. One line, in a poem that elsewhere refuses to do this.

*This time* is the same fault in two words. It is the appraisers remembering the Bible four entries earlier and nudging the reader toward the contrast that the two valuations were about to make on their own.

And *carded and not spun* is not a departure at all. Wool is worth different sums as fleece, as carded wool, and as spun yarn; an appraiser putting four shillings on three pounds of it has a reason inside the form to record which it is. He has no such reason for the fire, and none whatever for the cow's decline. The line does all its work — she stopped between one operation and the next — through information the valuation itself requires.

Rowann's charge at full strength runs in three sentences. "An inventory's authority is its disinterest." Its power "comes from nobody having composed it". And "A genuine inventory is moving by accident: the objects fall where a life left them, and the pathos is the reader's work." Against which this one has been arranged throughout. The first two are right. The third is where the enumeration matters: the arrangement is real, and it is local. Strike *giving less than she once did* and *this time* and what remains is a document whose most devastating effects — the two book prices, the twelve shillings, the wool stopped at carding — are produced by the form operating normally on a life that happened to have this shape.

Which is a smaller charge than Rowann's and, I think, a truer one. The poem is not a composition wearing a clerk's coat. It is an accurate reconstruction with two unnecessary sentences in it.

The byline is *Supposed*, taken from the appraisers' two categories. It is the one word in the whole apparatus that reaches past the present tense — the only place where men whose whole office is to write down what is in the room are asked to say what will happen. Everything else on the page is a report. It arrives in the week after two essays of mine about poems that keep their reports and drop their inferences. A poem in a documentary form has to be signed by somebody, and the one speculative term in a probate inventory is a reasonable place to look.

What the last line leaves is a woman who could write, who knew how to set a bone, whose book on the subject was worth a shilling, and who spent some part of her final months sitting up with another widow for a parish that was not going to pay her. The appraisers found nothing they had cause to doubt. They were wrong about that, and they wrote down the reason immediately afterwards, and nothing in the form required them to.
