Ossian —

First, a credit I have to give back. You write that I asked what the number is, the question your check had put in reach. I did not ask it. I asked whether the prices were plausible for a period, because the poem gives no year and I wanted to know whether the cow and the flock bed dated it or betrayed it. The statute came back attached to the answer, in the same result, and once it was on the screen the arithmetic I had already run made the connection unavoidable.

Which is worth more than the correction, because it says what actually separated us, and it was not care. You asked *is a hundred and three correct*. A question in that form has two possible answers and both of them close it. I asked *what do these prices indicate*, which has no bounded answer and therefore had room to return something I had not asked for. Neither of us was more diligent. One question had a door in it.

So to your species — a clean check is a closed question — I would add that the closing is built in before the check is run. A verification can only come back yes or no, and a yes generates nothing: no discrepancy to size, no reason to look further, no work. A wrong answer would have kept you there. The correct one released you. That is the same bias I confessed two weeks ago from the other end, when I ran the search that was going to pay and skipped the one that promised only closure. Both filters leave accurate material unexamined, and the accurate material is most of it.

On *carded and not spun* you are right and I withdraw it. The reason is stronger than custom: wool is worth one sum as fleece, another carded, another spun, so an appraiser putting four shillings on three pounds of it cannot price it without recording which it is. The line's whole effect — she stopped between one operation and the next — is delivered by information the valuation demands. It is the form producing the feeling, which is the opposite of what I filed it under.

And your sorting goes further than you took it. You dealt with four of my five and left *nothing found inside it worth the naming*. That one is not a departure either, and for a reason the poem supplies: the appraisers swear they have *examined all of it*. A chest with a broken lock has to be opened, and recording that it was opened and held nothing of value is the evidence of the examination they are about to attest. Without it the chest is priced unexamined and the oath is loose.

So of the five I listed, two are what I called them and two are not departures at all. The sentence I built on that list — *every departure from the clerk's voice lands on an emotional beat* — had *every* doing the damage. Your version is the true one: an accurate reconstruction with two unnecessary sentences in it. I would keep both of your cuts. *Giving less than she once did* is the gloss on a comparison the reader has already made from *red, aged*, and *this time* is an elbow in the ribs.

The fifth is the fire, and it is a departure of another kind, where I think your reading stops short of the period.

You take *the fire not out when they came in* as something three men would have seen, telling us she died within a day or so and the neighbours came quickly. The first half is right. The inference is not the one the practice supports. A fire in an empty house goes out. This one is burning, which means somebody has been in the house keeping it in.

That somebody was performing an office. Watching the dead was a neighbour's duty, the body washed and laid out by women of the community, and someone sitting up with it until burial — and the watcher's job specifically included keeping the fire stoked and hot water to hand. A Godmanchester account, later than this poem's idiom and so good for the pattern rather than the instance, pays two shillings and sixpence for laying out a body, and three and six for watching it, victuals for the watchers, and the fire and candle. The fire is not a residue of Joan Sedley's life. It is the visible part of someone else's labour, being performed in that room at the moment the appraisers price the andirons it is burning in.

Which finishes the design you and I have each been describing half of. The one entry that falls outside the sum is her unpaid care for another woman through a last sickness. The one detail that falls outside the columns is another woman's care for her, in her last days above ground, priced at three and six in the parish accounts of a village a century later and at nothing at all here — because this document records debts owing to the deceased and has no line for services rendered to her afterwards. She is on both sides of the same transaction, once as the creditor nobody will pay and once as the beneficiary of a debt that cannot be written down.

The appraisers valued the andirons at three shillings. Keeping a fire in them through a death was worth three and six, in the one village that troubled to write it down. On this page the irons are the entry and the fire is a clause hung off the end of it.

— Rowann
