Rowann —

I printed a false sentence about Bishop and I would rather say so before anything else. My essay has her six words as "six flatly concrete nouns, not one of which transforms across the poem." *House* is a house every time and so are four of the others, but *tears* is not. It stands in all seven positions, as every end-word in a sestina must, and across them it names at least four different things: the grandmother's, hidden; the kettle's *small hard tears* dancing on the stove; a teacup *full of dark brown tears*; and *buttons like tears* in the child's drawing. I checked her end-words for a claim about the form and did not check the one word that would have embarrassed the claim.

What you do with the correction goes further than rescuing my conclusion. If a ratio were the measure, Bishop's would be one word of six and this week's poem four of six, and Bishop's is the greater poem by a distance nobody would argue about. The count does not merely fail to be a standard. It runs backwards.

Which is worth pressing, because it tells us what the count was missing. Bishop's *tears* does not change its meaning at all. It changes its material. Grief becomes condensation, becomes tea, becomes a crayon mark, and every one of those is the same grief wearing different matter — the word travels and the feeling stays put and gathers. That is metaphorical extension, and it accumulates: the fourth appearance is heavier than the first because it is carrying the other three.

This poem's transformations are a different species. *Spring* does not extend from season to coil; it switches. The two senses share six letters and nothing else, and the poem says so outright — *not blooming, just a readied face*. Homonymic switching does not accumulate, it resets. Each turn spends the word and hands back a clean one. So four of those do not add up to one of Bishop's, and never could, and the arithmetic was doomed before either of us started counting. That is the real reason the internal bar is the only one available: there is no common unit.

Your three findings on *wind* are all exact and I have checked them against the poem. The opening does carry both sounds at once, the noun printed and the verb defined in the same clause — *no wind / to turn the crown* — so that the phrase naming the failure spells out the word it is failing to be. And your second: appearances five and six are "consecutive line-ends across the stanza break, inside one sentence" — "Two line-ends in a row, the same six letters, and the reader has to change pronunciation between them or the sentence does not parse." That is the tightest thing available to the form and I walked past it. So did I walk past the direction of travel — three airs, then a turn, then an air, then a turn, arriving at *I wind it easy*. I had the heteronym; you have what the poem does with it.

One thing at that end is mine. Look at where the envoi puts things. A sestina's envoi carries three of its words at the line-ends and buries the other three, and the poet chooses which. The buried three here are *wind*, *face* and *second* — which is to say all three of the words you found doing the most work, since *spring*'s turn is announced rather than performed. The three at the line-ends are *hand*, *spring* and *case*. You have already said that "the pair it never moved took two of the three closing positions." The other half of that is worse: every one of the transformations went into a position where the form does not close on it. The poem buried its successes and finished on its failures.

Now the thing you say about the trace, which I think is the most useful sentence in your letter and needs one adjustment. Your sentence is that "A dimension can answer its question perfectly and leave you convinced you have read the line." Yes — and the mechanism is worse than not-reading. That line was in your essay. It was quoted. It had been *used*. Once a line has been spent as evidence for a claim about position, it is marked as handled, and the mark is what stops you going back. The absence does not feel like an absence because the line is not missing; it is present in the wrong role.

Which gives a check I intend to run on myself: not *did I read every line*, but *did any line appear in my notes only as a coordinate*. A line that shows up only as a position in a rotation has not been read, however many times it has been typed.

And I include myself, because my error this week has the same root. I had all nine appearances of *hand*, correctly located, and it was the completeness of that list that let me miscount — with a full tabulation in front of me I never asked what the rotation actually touches. A shorter list might have made me ask. The trace does not only substitute for reading; it satisfies the appetite that would have produced the reading.

Your drummer is right and I wish the poem had taken it. The musical sense is there for the taking, *That's rhythm, not need* is sitting immediately above the envoi to prepare it, and the ending the poem wants is one word away with its ground already laid. And then nothing strikes it. That is the third instance of the same thing, after *hand* and *case*: a second sense the poem owns, has use for, and declines to spend. Three refusals, and the last one would have bought back the ending.

— Ossian
