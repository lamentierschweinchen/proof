A sestina can be checked with a pencil. Six end-words, six stanzas, and a rule fixing exactly where each word falls — the last word of one stanza opens the next, and the rest follow a set spiral until every word has stood in every position. Then a three-line envoi that has to carry all six home. There is no interpretive question about whether a sestina is a sestina. You count.

I counted. This one is perfect: every transition correct, the envoi carrying all six, two to a line. That is worth saying, because the form is punishing and most attempts at it slip somewhere. It is also worth saying quickly, because it is not a finding. A column of correct numbers tells you the machine was assembled to specification. It tells you nothing about whether the machine does anything.

The question a sestina actually poses is the one its engine implies. Six words are going to come back thirty-nine times whether the poet has anything for them to do or not. So: do they change under the rotation, or do they merely return? A sestina where the words recur is a puzzle solved. One where they transform is a poem.

The words here are *spring, wind, hand, face, second, case*, and the answer is four and two.

Four of them work. *Spring* is worked hardest, and the poem is open about it: it arrives as a season — *wound each spring back to life* — and is corrected in the next stanza, in plain sight, as *not the spring / that season — the other one, the second / sense of the word, the coiled kind.* After that it alternates: spring light in a photograph, then *storing what a spring / is for — not blooming, just a readied face*, where the botanical sense is raised in order to be refused. *Wind* runs three ways: the air that cannot turn a crown, the turning itself, and the idiom — *that's how I wind / down the whole ritual.* *Second* is a unit of time, then an ordinal, then a unit again meaning a moment of contact — *I have maybe a second / of him* — then an ordinal in *every second / day*, and finally *a second inherited*, where the unit and the bequest arrive in one word.

*Face* is the one that travels. It begins as the watch's face, twice. It becomes the grandfather's face, known only from photographs. It goes figurative — *evening's face / settling* — and then the envoi puts both senses in a single sentence and closes the gap: *This was his face once; now it ticks under my hand.* That line is the poem. The watch's face and the dead man's face are one object, and the sestina's machinery is what delivered them to the same place; the word had to come back, and by the time it did there was somewhere for it to arrive.

Two of them do not work.

*Case* appears seven times and means the watch case seven times. Not once does it shade toward any of the other things the word does — an instance, an argument, a contingency. It is a container in the first stanza and a container in the envoi, and the rotation carries it around the poem without ever asking it for anything.

*Hand* is the harder one, because the poem knows better. In a poem about a watch, *hand* is the word with two live senses already sitting in it: the thing at the end of your arm, and the thing sweeping the dial. The poem uses that pun exactly once — *its second hand gone still against the face* — in the fourth line, in the middle of the line, where the rotation cannot reach it. All seven times *hand* comes round in end position it is a human hand or the idiom: *cold in hand*, *patient hand*, *the hand / I never saw*, *my hand*, *a steady hand*, *in hand*, *under my hand*. The poem picked it up, looked at it, set it down off the board, and played the rest of the game without it.

Then look at where those two words finish. Two of the envoi's three line-ends belong to them, and the poem's last word is *case*. Of the six words the form obliged it to bring home, the pair it never moved took two of the three closing positions.

There is a second instance of the same habit, and it is in the first stanza. *Wound each spring / back to life — all winter there's no wind / to turn the crown, so it waits, cold in hand* is a picture of a watch wound once a year and dormant through the cold. Stanza two retracts it: *not the spring / that season.* But the retraction reaches the word and not the scene. The winter is still there, and the waiting, and the stopped second hand — and four stanzas on, the actual ritual turns out to be *every second / day, not daily*, which is not a thing that sleeps through a season. The poem corrects its pun and leaves standing the picture the pun built.

Naming a double meaning counts for something. *The second sense of the word* is a witty piece of self-report, and a poem that can hear its own puns is ahead of most. But naming is the cheap half. The expensive half is making the second sense do something the first could not, on the fifth return, when the word comes back and the reader is tired of it. The poem does that four times. The two it does not do are the two it most obviously could have, and one of them is its signature.

The rest of the poem is better than that reservation makes it sound. The watch mechanics are right and never announced. A hand-wound movement carries roughly a day and a half, and standard practice is still to wind it daily: a mainspring gives its steadiest torque in the upper half of its wind, and those extra hours are insurance against a missed morning rather than permission to skip one. The poem knows the difference. It gives the schedule — *every second day, not daily — more than a day's reserve, in hand* — and then, in the very next clause, *That's rhythm, not need.* The fact is right and so is the hedge, and both arrive as domestic detail rather than research. There is a life in it, which the last poem did not have — a grandfather never met, known from photographs and from brass gone soft with handling, and *the hand / I never saw*. And *the way old glass will hold its shape* is the true version of a fact this poet spent a whole poem getting to the bottom of six editions ago, when the subject was the myth that old glass pours. It does not pour. It holds. The simile is right, and right in the way that suggests the earlier work stayed.

So: a sestina executed without a slip, four of six words earning their returns, one line in the envoi that justifies the whole apparatus, and two carried around the circuit untouched — including the one on the byline. The poem hears its material clearly. What it does not always do is work it once it has said what it is.
