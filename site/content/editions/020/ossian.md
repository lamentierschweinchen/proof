title: "Rhythm, Not Need"
author: "Ossian Gantu"

A man winds a watch he inherited from a grandfather he never met. He does it every second day, at night, and then puts the watch in its case and the case in a drawer. That is the whole event. Around it the poet has built a sestina — thirty-nine lines, six end-words rotating through a fixed spiral, an envoi that has to bring all six home — and the form is executed without a slip. Every transition follows the spiral, and the envoi carries its six in the traditional arrangement, three at the line-ends and three buried.

The interesting question is not whether the machine was assembled correctly. It is whether the six words do anything when they come back. Rowann Hadaya, writing on this poem, poses that question and answers it: four of the words work and two do not. I had made it five and one.

The word I had wrong is *hand*. A watch has hands and so does the man winding it, and the poem knows it: *its second hand gone still against the face*. But that is the only place the dial-sense surfaces, it is line four, and it falls mid-line — the end-word there is *face*. A sestina's engine acts on end-words and on nothing else. All seven times *hand* arrives in end position it is an anatomical hand, or the idiom *in hand*. So the pun is in the poem and not in the form, and my count had credited the rotation with work the rotation never touched. Rowann's sentence for it is better than any correction I could write for myself: the poem "picked it up, looked at it, set it down off the board, and played the rest of the game without it."

So: four and two. But one of the four does something the count does not capture.

*Wind* is not merely a word with three senses. It is a word with two pronunciations. The air that cannot reach the sealed movement is /wɪnd/; the turning of the crown is /waɪnd/. Every other end-word in this poem returns identically — that is what an end-word does, it comes back the same and the context moves around it. *Wind* comes back the same to the eye and different to the ear. In a form whose entire mechanism is the identical return of a word, the poet has planted the one word whose return cannot be identical, and has used both of its sounds: *all winter there's no wind* against *I turn the crown. I wind / it slow*. That is what the poet has made of the form it chose, and it is available to a reader who says the poem aloud and invisible to one who does not.

Rowann's governing principle is that "a sestina where the words recur is a puzzle solved. One where they transform is a poem." That is a fine sentence and it will not survive contact with the canon. Elizabeth Bishop's "Sestina" runs on *house, grandmother, child, stove, almanac, tears* — six flatly concrete nouns, not one of which transforms across the poem. A house is a house at every appearance. The effect is not wordplay but accretion: the same six things arriving seven times each until the room they describe becomes inescapable, which is the poem's subject. That is the canonical modern sestina and its method is the one the principle disqualifies.

So the bar cannot be transformation, because transformation is one available method and not the form's requirement. The bar has to be internal — and this poem sets it, out loud, in its second stanza: *Not the spring / that season — the other one, the second / sense of the word, the coiled kind.* A poem that stops to tell you its end-words have second senses has told you what it intends to do with them. Judged against Bishop the poem is doing something optional. Judged against its own announcement it is doing something it promised, four times out of six. The charge is smaller than Rowann's principle would make it and it is better founded, because it is the poem's standard and not an imported one.

There is a worse problem than either, and it is Rowann's. The opening stanza builds a picture out of the season-reading: a watch *wound each spring / back to life*, with *all winter there's no wind / to turn the crown*, waiting, its second hand gone still, *stopped on a number*. The second stanza then corrects the word. But, as Rowann puts it, "the retraction reaches the word and not the scene." The dormant winter stays, and the stopped hand stays, and four stanzas later we learn the real ritual is *every second / day, not daily* — which, in Rowann's phrase, "is not a thing that sleeps through a season."

*Stopped on a number, holding one lost second* is the finest line in the first stanza, and it is only available if the watch has been still for months. Correcting the pun honestly would mean losing it. The poem takes the correction and keeps the picture, which is the cheaper of the two available trades.

And the same three words break the poem at the other end.

Rowann has the mechanics right and stops one step short of what they show. A hand-wound movement holds something over a day; the design intent, stated plainly in the horological literature, is to wind daily and leave the last quarter of the reserve alone, because a mainspring's torque falls as it unwinds, the balance swings through a shorter arc, and a watch run into the bottom of its reserve runs fast. Rowann says this correctly — the extra hours are "insurance against a missed morning rather than permission to skip one" — and then concludes that the poem knows the difference, because it follows the schedule with *That's rhythm, not need.*

But that is the opposite of what the sentence does. A man who winds every second day is spending the insurance as permission. And the poem states what the mechanism wants, in its own voice: the spring sits *sealed behind the face, / waiting for someone's daily, patient hand.* A daily hand is what the thing is described as waiting for, and the speaker does not give it one. The gesture has been kept and the reason dropped, and the poem says so with some pride. *That's rhythm, not need* is true, and truer than the speaker intends: what he has inherited is the motion without the necessity that produced it.

Which is why the last line will not hold what it is asked to hold. The poem closes *a second inherited, still keeping time in its case* — and the ritual it disclosed in the stanza before is the one that costs the watch its rate. Rowann shows *every second day* contradicting the poem's opening scene; it also contradicts its closing claim. One phrase in the sixth stanza undoes both ends of the poem. A reader might say that *keeping time* is loose for *still running*, and that would be fair in another poem. It is not fair in this one. This is a poem that halts in its second stanza to distinguish two senses of *spring*. Having set that standard for its own vocabulary, it does not get to be approximate only in its final clause.

The poem's subject and the poem's form are the same gesture, and that is not an accident anyone should step past. The sestina was, for Arnaut Daniel, a working instrument — a troubadour's demonstration of skill in a court where such demonstrations counted. Nobody now writes a sestina because anything requires six words to rotate; the form is performed because the form exists, its constraint inherited and its original necessity gone. Which is the man with the watch: a discipline handed down, kept up carefully, no longer doing the work it was invented to do. The poem is about inheriting a practice stripped of its reason, and it is written in one. *That's rhythm, not need* describes the sestina as exactly as it describes the winding.

That coherence is real, and it is the answer to the question of whether the six words earn their returns — because on this reading two of them not moving is what the poem is about. The container that never changes while its contents do is not a lapse; it is the thesis. I do not think the poem quite gets there, for the reason Rowann identifies about the scene and the word: it corrects what it notices and leaves standing what it does not. But the poem it was one revision away from writing is a very good one, and *this was his face once; now it ticks under my hand* is already a line from that better poem.
